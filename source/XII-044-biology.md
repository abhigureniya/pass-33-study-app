# CBSE Class XII Biology (044) — Pass Pack 2026-27 (English medium)

For students who want to make sure they **pass**. Everything here follows the NCERT Class XII Biology book and the CBSE 2026-27 curriculum, Sample Question Paper (SQP) and Marking Scheme (MS).

How to use this pack:
1. Read Section 1 and 3 first. You will see that passing Biology is very possible.
2. Study the chapters in the order of the 33-mark plan (Section 3).
3. Learn the questions in Section 5. They have come again and again in board papers.
4. Practise the diagrams in Section 6 at least 3 times each.

Extra marks without extra chapters: in Section A, **attempt all 16 MCQs/A-R** — there is no negative marking. Even guessing among 4 options gives you a chance.

## 5. Must-do question bank (103 questions)

Tags: (2019 OD) = Outside Delhi paper 2019; (2023 D) = Delhi 2023; (SQP 26-27) = CBSE Sample Paper 2026-27. "Similar" means the same idea was asked with different wording.

### Chapter 1 — Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

**Q1. (MCQ)** In a plant, the pistil matures several days before the stamens in all flowers. The most important result is that it: (A) guarantees self-pollination (B) promotes autogamy (C) ensures cross-pollination (D) leads to geitonogamy. *(SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** (C) It ensures cross-pollination.

**Q2. (MCQ)** An angiosperm embryo sac is located within the: (A) placenta (B) megasporangium (C) nucellus (D) ovary. *(2024)*
**Answer:** (C) Nucellus.

**Q3. (MCQ)** An anther has 50 microspore mother cells and the ovary has 50 megaspore mother cells. Number of pollen grains and ovules likely formed: (A) 100, 25 (B) 200, 50 (C) 50, 50 (D) 200, 100. *(2025)*
**Answer:** (B) 200, 50. (Each microspore mother cell → 4 pollen grains by meiosis; each megaspore mother cell → only 1 functional megaspore → 1 embryo sac.)

**Q4. (2 marks)** A mature embryo sac has 7 cells but 8 nuclei. Explain. *(2017 OD; similar 2019 D, 2020 D, 2023 D)*
**Answer:** The functional megaspore divides 3 times (free nuclear mitosis) → 8 nuclei. 3 nuclei at the micropylar end form the egg apparatus (2 synergids + 1 egg cell); 3 at the chalazal end form 3 antipodal cells; the remaining 2 nuclei (polar nuclei) stay together in one large central cell. So: 3 + 3 + 1 central cell = 7 cells, 8 nuclei.

**Q5. (3 marks)** Explain the monosporic development of the embryo sac. *(2023 D; similar 2020 D)*
**Answer:** (1) The megaspore mother cell (MMC, 2n) in the nucellus undergoes meiosis → 4 haploid megaspores. (2) 3 megaspores degenerate; only 1 is functional — so it is called **monosporic** development. (3) The functional megaspore nucleus divides by mitosis 3 times (free nuclear, no walls) → 2, 4, 8 nuclei; then cell walls form → 7-celled, 8-nucleate embryo sac (female gametophyte).

**Q6. (2 marks)** Explain the development of the male gametophyte. Why is it called a male gametophyte? *(2023 OD; similar 2017 D, 2025)*
**Answer:** The microspore (pollen grain, n) divides by mitosis into a big **vegetative cell** (food store, irregular nucleus) and a small **generative cell** (spindle shaped, floats in vegetative cell). In ~60% of angiosperms pollen is shed at this 2-celled stage; in others the generative cell divides again into **2 male gametes** (3-celled stage). It is called the male gametophyte because it is the haploid structure that produces the male gametes.

**Q7. (2–3 marks)** List and explain outbreeding devices. *(2018 OD, 2023 OD, 2024, 2026)*
**Answer (any three):**
1. **Pollen release and stigma receptivity not synchronised** — pollen is released before the stigma is ready, or the stigma is ready much before pollen release (SQP Q1 case).
2. **Anther and stigma at different positions** — pollen cannot reach the stigma of the same flower.
3. **Self-incompatibility** — a genetic mechanism that stops pollen of the same flower (or plant) from germinating or growing a tube in the pistil.
4. **Unisexual flowers** — monoecious plants (castor, maize) prevent autogamy but not geitonogamy; dioecious plants (papaya) prevent both autogamy and geitonogamy.

**Q8. (2 marks)** Geitonogamy can be compared with both cross-pollination and self-pollination. Comment. *(SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** Geitonogamy = pollen from the anther of one flower goes to the stigma of **another flower of the same plant**. Functionally it is like cross-pollination because a pollinating agent is needed; genetically it is like autogamy (self-pollination) because the pollen comes from the same plant.

**Q9. (3 marks)** Explain the steps a plant breeder takes so that only the desired pollen reaches the stigma of a bisexual flower (artificial hybridisation). *(2023 D; similar 2015 OD, 2019 OD, SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** (1) **Emasculation** — remove the anthers from the flower bud with forceps before the anthers dehisce. (2) **Bagging** — cover the emasculated flower with a butter-paper bag to stop unwanted pollen. (3) When the stigma becomes receptive, dust it with pollen collected from the chosen male parent, and **re-bag** the flower so the fruit develops. (In unisexual flowers like papaya, no emasculation is needed — only bagging.)

**Q10. (5 marks)** Explain double fertilisation, starting from pollen on the stigma. Give the ploidy of the products and the role of synergids. Why does endosperm develop before the embryo? *(2024; similar 2018 OD, SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:**
- Pollen grain germinates on the stigma → pollen tube grows through the style → enters the ovule through the micropyle → enters one **synergid** (guided by the **filiform apparatus** of synergids) → releases 2 male gametes.
- **Syngamy:** male gamete 1 + egg cell → **zygote (2n)** → embryo.
- **Triple fusion:** male gamete 2 + 2 polar nuclei in central cell → **primary endosperm nucleus, PEN (3n)** → endosperm.
- Two fusions happen in one embryo sac, so it is called **double fertilisation**.
- **Endosperm develops first** because its cells store food, which nourishes the developing embryo. (SQP MS: the statement "zygote always divides before PEN" is false.)

**Q11. (2 marks)** Why do farmers prefer apomictic seeds? *(2020 D, 2026; similar 2015 OD, 2019 OD)*
**Answer:** **Apomixis** = formation of seed without fertilisation (example: some Asteraceae and grasses). (1) If a hybrid is made apomictic, the hybrid characters do not segregate in the next generation. (2) So farmers can keep using their own seeds every year instead of buying costly hybrid seeds each year.

**Q12. (2 marks)** When an orange seed is squeezed, many embryos are seen. Explain. Are they genetically similar? *(2017 D)*
**Answer:** This is **polyembryony**. In citrus, some nucellar cells around the embryo sac divide and grow into extra embryos (without fertilisation). These nucellar embryos are genetically identical to the mother plant (clones); only the zygotic embryo differs.

**Q13. (2 marks)** Differentiate between true fruit and false fruit, and give an example of a parthenocarpic fruit. *(2020 OD; similar 2018 OD, 2026)*
**Answer:** True fruit develops only from the ovary (e.g., mango). False fruit — the thalamus also contributes to the fruit (e.g., apple, strawberry, cashew). Parthenocarpic fruit develops without fertilisation and is seedless (e.g., banana).

### Chapter 2 — Human Reproduction

**Q14. (MCQ)** In humans, the secondary oocyte completes its meiotic division when: (A) it is implanted (B) it is released from the Graafian follicle (C) it is penetrated by the sperm (D) acrosomal enzymes break the zona pellucida. *(2024)*
**Answer:** (C) When the sperm enters it.

**Q15. (MCQ)** The foetal ejection reflex triggers release of ____ from ____. (A) oxytocin, foetal pituitary (B) oxytocin, maternal pituitary (C) hCG, placenta (D) progesterone, corpus luteum. *(2026; similar 2015 D)*
**Answer:** (B) Oxytocin, maternal pituitary.

**Q16. (Assertion–Reason)** A: The embryo with 8 to 16 blastomeres is called a morula. R: The morula continues to divide and transforms into trophoblast. *(2026)*
**Answer:** (C) A is true but R is false. (The morula becomes a **blastocyst**; trophoblast is only its outer layer.)

**Q17. (Assertion–Reason)** A: Spermatogenesis is continuous throughout a man's reproductive life, while oogenesis is discontinuous. R: Oogenesis begins in the embryo, is arrested at prophase I, resumes after puberty, and is arrested again at metaphase II. *(SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** (A) Both true, R is the correct explanation of A.

**Q18. (2 marks)** List the male accessory ducts in order. *(2020 OD; 2019 OD diagram; 2024 case)*
**Answer:** Rete testis → vasa efferentia → epididymis → vas deferens (→ ejaculatory duct → urethra).

**Q19. (3 marks)** Explain spermatogenesis and the roles of LH and FSH. *(2024 case, 2020 D)*
**Answer:** In seminiferous tubules: spermatogonia (2n) multiply by mitosis → **primary spermatocytes (2n)** → meiosis I → **secondary spermatocytes (n)** → meiosis II → **spermatids (n)** → change into **sperms** (spermiogenesis). At puberty the hypothalamus releases GnRH → pituitary releases **LH**, which acts on **Leydig (interstitial) cells** to secrete androgens (testosterone) that stimulate spermatogenesis, and **FSH**, which acts on **Sertoli cells** to secrete factors that help spermiogenesis.

**Q20. (3–5 marks)** Describe the menstrual cycle phases. *(2016 OD, 2019 D, 2025, 2026, SQP 26-27 case)*
**Answer:**
- **Menstrual phase (days 1–5):** endometrium breaks down and bleeds out; happens only if the ovum is not fertilised.
- **Follicular/proliferative phase (days ~5–13):** FSH and LH rise; primary follicles grow into a mature **Graafian follicle**; follicle secretes **estrogen**, which rebuilds the endometrium.
- **Ovulation (about day 14):** **LH surge** causes the Graafian follicle to rupture and release the ovum (secondary oocyte).
- **Luteal/secretory phase (days 15–28):** the empty follicle becomes the **corpus luteum**, which secretes **progesterone** to keep the endometrium ready for implantation. If no fertilisation, the corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → menstruation starts again.
(SQP link: no clear LH surge → no ovulation → no corpus luteum → low progesterone → irregular, heavy bleeding; heavy bleeding can cause **anaemia**.)

**Q21. (3–5 marks)** Trace the journey of the zygote from the fallopian tube to implantation. *(2023 OD; similar 2024, 2025, 2026)*
**Answer:** Fertilisation occurs in the **ampullary region** of the fallopian tube. The zygote divides by mitosis (**cleavage**) as it moves through the isthmus towards the uterus → 2, 4, 8, 16 daughter cells (**blastomeres**). Embryo with 8–16 blastomeres = **morula**. Morula becomes a **blastocyst**: outer layer **trophoblast** + inner group of cells, the **inner cell mass**. The trophoblast attaches to the endometrium; uterine cells divide rapidly and cover the blastocyst → blastocyst gets embedded = **implantation** → pregnancy. Inner cell mass becomes the embryo; trophoblast helps in nutrition and implantation (SQP Q3).

**Q22. (2 marks)** How is polyspermy prevented? *(2024 OR)*
**Answer:** When one sperm touches the zona pellucida of the ovum, changes occur in the zona pellucida that block the entry of other sperms.

**Q23. (2 marks)** Why is breast-feeding recommended in the first months? *(2016 D, 2018 OD)*
**Answer:** The first milk, **colostrum**, contains many antibodies (IgA) that give the baby passive immunity, and breast milk gives complete nutrition and builds a healthy baby.

### Chapter 3 — Reproductive Health

**Q24. (MCQ)** A man undergoes an operation in which a small part of the vas deferens is cut and tied. The direct result is: (A) sperm transport into ejaculatory duct and urethra is blocked (B) testosterone stops (C) seminal plasma stops (D) spermatogenesis stops. *(SQP 26-27; similar 2023 OD, 2023 D)*
**Answer:** (A). This is **vasectomy** (tubectomy is the similar operation in females).

**Q25. (2 marks)** Name two copper-releasing IUDs and state how they work. *(2024; similar 2020 D)*
**Answer:** CuT, Cu7, Multiload 375 (any two). Cu ions reduce sperm motility and the fertilising capacity of sperms; IUDs also increase phagocytosis of sperms in the uterus. (Hormone-releasing IUDs like Progestasert and LNG-20 also make the uterus unsuitable for implantation and the cervix hostile to sperms.)

**Q26. (2 marks)** Why is "Saheli" preferred by women? *(2020 D)*
**Answer:** It is a non-steroidal pill taken **once a week**, with very few side effects and high contraceptive value. (Developed by CDRI, Lucknow.)

**Q27. (2 marks)** What is amniocentesis? Why is it banned for sex determination? *(2026; similar 2019 OD, 2023 D, 2025)*
**Answer:** A sample of amniotic fluid around the foetus is taken and tested to detect **genetic/chromosomal disorders** (such as Down's syndrome). It is legally banned for finding the sex of the foetus because this misuse leads to **female foeticide** (killing of normal female foetuses).

**Q28. (2 marks)** Why does the government have strict conditions for MTP? *(2017 OD)*
**Answer:** To stop illegal, unsafe abortions and the misuse of MTP for female foeticide (sex-selective abortion). MTP is safest in the first trimester (up to 12 weeks).

**Q29. (3 marks)** A woman has blocked fallopian tubes but her ovaries make normal ova. Her husband has a very low sperm count. Which ARTs will help? *(SQP 26-27; similar 2015 D, 2019 D, 2020 OD, 2023 OD, 2024)*
**Answer:** (1) **ICSI (Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection)** — a single sperm is injected directly into the ovum in the lab; solves the low sperm count. (2) **IVF followed by IUT (Intra Uterine Transfer)** — embryo made in the lab (with more than 8 blastomeres) is placed directly into the uterus; this by-passes the blocked fallopian tube.

**Q30. (3 marks)** Expand and explain ZIFT, GIFT and IUI. *(2023 OD; similar 2019 D, 2024)*
**Answer:**
- **ZIFT – Zygote Intra Fallopian Transfer:** zygote or early embryo (up to 8 blastomeres) made by IVF is put into the fallopian tube.
- **GIFT – Gamete Intra Fallopian Transfer:** an ovum from a donor is put into the fallopian tube of a woman who cannot produce ova but can give a suitable environment for fertilisation.
- **IUI – Intra Uterine Insemination (artificial insemination):** semen from the husband or a healthy donor is placed into the uterus; used when the man has a low sperm count.

**Q31. (2 marks)** Name any four STIs. Which of them are not completely curable? *(NCERT fact; not seen in the papers read — learn as a 2-mark backup)*
**Answer:** Gonorrhoea, syphilis, genital herpes, chlamydiasis, genital warts, trichomoniasis, hepatitis B, HIV/AIDS. Hepatitis B, genital herpes and HIV infections are not completely curable.

### Chapter 4 — Principles of Inheritance and Variation

**Q32. (MCQ)** A woman with normal vision has a colour-blind father. She marries a normal man. The chance of their children being colour blind is: (A) 25% (B) 50% (C) 75% (D) 100%. *(2024; similar 2025)*
**Answer:** (A) 25% of all children (= half of the sons; no daughters affected). Woman is a carrier XᶜX; man XY.

**Q33. (2 marks)** You have a tall pea plant. How will you find its genotype? *(2019 D; similar 2015 D, 2016 OD, 2017 D, 2023 D)*
**Answer:** Do a **test cross**: cross it with a dwarf (homozygous recessive, tt) plant.
- If all offspring are tall → plant is **TT** (homozygous).
- If tall : dwarf = **1 : 1** → plant is **Tt** (heterozygous).

**Q34. (5 marks)** Compare inheritance of flower colour in garden pea (violet/white) and snapdragon (red/white) up to F2. *(2024, 2025, 2026; similar 2019 OD)*
**Answer:**
- **Pea (complete dominance):** VV (violet) × vv (white) → F1 all **Vv violet**. F1 selfed → F2: VV : Vv : vv = 1 : 2 : 1 (genotype), violet : white = **3 : 1** (phenotype). Violet is dominant.
- **Snapdragon/Antirrhinum (incomplete dominance):** RR (red) × rr (white) → F1 all **Rr pink**. F2: RR red : Rr pink : rr white = **1 : 2 : 1** for both genotype and phenotype.
- **Conclusion:** In incomplete dominance neither allele is fully dominant, so F1 shows an intermediate character and the phenotypic ratio equals the genotypic ratio.

**Q35. (3 marks)** A woman with blood group O marries a man with blood group AB. Work out the cross and explain the pattern. *(2019 D; similar 2015 D, 2025)*
**Answer:** Woman ii × Man IᴬIᴮ. Gametes: i and Iᴬ, Iᴮ. Children: Iᴬi (blood group A) and Iᴮi (blood group B) in 1 : 1. No child is O or AB. ABO blood groups show **multiple alleles** (Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i) and **co-dominance** (Iᴬ and Iᴮ both express in AB); i is recessive.

**Q36. (3 marks)** Mice: running R > walking r; black B > brown b. Cross Rrbb female × RrBb male. Give the phenotypic ratio and number of running-black mice out of 64. *(SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** Gametes: female Rb, rb; male RB, Rb, rB, rb. Results: running black 6/16, running brown 6/16, walking black 2/16, walking brown 2/16 → **3 : 3 : 1 : 1**. Running black = 6/16 × 64 = **24**.

**Q37. (Assertion–Reason)** A: Tissue culture is used when traditional breeding cannot keep pace with demand. R: Totipotency is the ability of a single plant cell/explant to regenerate a whole plant under sterile conditions. *(SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** (B) Both true, but R is not the correct explanation of A. (One-line extra: a virus-free plant is obtained by culturing the **meristem** (apical/axillary), which is free of virus — 2026.)

**Q38. (Assertion–Reason)** A: When two genes in a dihybrid cross are on the same chromosome, parental combinations are much higher than non-parental. R: This is due to crossing over between the two genes. *(2025; similar SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** (C) A is true, R is false. (High parental combinations are due to **linkage**; crossing over produces the non-parental recombinants.)

**Q39. (3 marks)** Explain linkage and recombination from Morgan's work. *(2018 OD, 2019 OD, 2020 D)*
**Answer:** Morgan worked with *Drosophila melanogaster* (short life cycle ~2 weeks, many offspring, easy to grow, clear male/female differences, many visible variations). In dihybrid crosses for X-linked genes, F2 ratios differed greatly from 9:3:3:1. Genes on the same chromosome tend to stay together = **linkage**; new (non-parental) combinations formed by crossing over = **recombination**. Tightly linked genes (yellow body and white eye) gave very low recombination (1.3%); loosely linked genes (white eye and miniature wing) gave higher recombination (37.2%). Sturtevant used recombination frequency to map gene positions on chromosomes.

**Q40. (2 marks)** Explain sex determination in honeybees. Why does a male bee have 16 and a female 32 chromosomes? *(2015 OD, 2016 OD, 2020 OD)*
**Answer:** **Haplodiploid** system: a fertilised egg becomes a female (queen or worker), diploid, 32 chromosomes; an unfertilised egg develops by **parthenogenesis** into a male (drone), haploid, 16 chromosomes. Drones produce sperms by **mitosis**. Males have no father and cannot have sons, but have a grandfather.

**Q41. (2 marks)** How is sex determined in birds and how is it different from humans? *(2018 OD; similar 2015 D)*
**Answer:** Birds: female is **ZW** (heterogametic, gives two types of eggs), male is **ZZ**. Humans: male is **XY** (heterogametic, two types of sperms), female is **XX**. So in birds the female decides the sex of the chick; in humans the father's sperm decides it.

**Q42. (2 marks)** Why do males suffer from haemophilia more than females? Give two differences between haemophilia and sickle-cell anaemia. *(2020 OD, 2024; similar 2017 D)*
**Answer:** Haemophilia is **X-linked recessive**. A male has only one X, so one defective allele makes him ill. A female needs the defective allele on both X chromosomes, which is very rare (mother carrier + father haemophilic). Differences: haemophilia is sex-linked, more males affected, blood does not clot (a clotting protein is missing); sickle-cell anaemia is **autosomal recessive**, males and females equally affected, caused by glutamic acid → valine change at the 6th position of the β-globin chain (GAG → GUG), RBCs become sickle shaped.

**Q43. (3 marks)** How does a single gene mutation cause phenylketonuria (PKU)? What is pleiotropy? *(SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** PKU is an autosomal recessive inborn error of metabolism. The person lacks the enzyme that converts **phenylalanine to tyrosine**. Phenylalanine accumulates and is changed into **phenylpyruvic acid** and other derivatives; their build-up in the brain causes mental retardation (they are also excreted in urine). **Pleiotropy:** one gene shows many phenotypic effects (here: mental retardation, reduced hair and skin pigmentation, etc.), because it affects a metabolic pathway.

**Q44. (3 marks)** Chromosomal vs Mendelian disorders; describe Down's, Turner's and Klinefelter's syndromes. *(2026; similar 2017 OD, 2019 D, 2024, 2025)*
**Answer:** Mendelian disorder = change/mutation in a **single gene** (haemophilia, sickle cell, thalassemia, PKU, colour blindness). Chromosomal disorder = absence, excess or abnormal arrangement of **whole chromosomes**, usually due to **non-disjunction** (failure of chromatids/chromosomes to separate in meiosis).
- **Down's syndrome:** trisomy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes). Short stature, small round head, furrowed tongue, partially open mouth, broad palm with a characteristic crease, retarded physical, psychomotor and mental development.
- **Klinefelter's syndrome:** 44 + XXY (47). Overall masculine but with feminine development (breast development, gynaecomastia), sterile. (Formed when an XX egg is fertilised by a Y sperm.)
- **Turner's syndrome:** 44 + XO (45). Female, sterile (rudimentary ovaries), lacks secondary sexual characters.

### Chapter 5 — Molecular Basis of Inheritance

**Q45. (MCQ)** A DNA molecule is 160 base pairs long and has 30% guanine. How many adenine bases? (A) 48 (B) 64 (C) 96 (D) 192. *(2026)*
**Answer:** (B) 64. Steps: 160 bp = 320 bases. G = 30%, so C = 30%. A + T = 40%, A = 20%. 20% of 320 = 64.

**Q46. (MCQ)** E. coli grown in ¹⁵N medium is moved to ¹⁴N medium. DNA after 60 minutes (E. coli divides every 20 minutes) will be: (A) 25% hybrid, 75% light (B) 50%/50% (C) 75% hybrid, 25% light (D) 85%/15%. *(SQP 26-27; similar 2016 OD, 2016 D, 2020 OD)*
**Answer:** (A). Steps: 60 min = 3 generations. Gen 1: all hybrid; Gen 2: ½ hybrid ½ light; Gen 3: ¼ hybrid ¾ light. This proved **semi-conservative replication** (Meselson and Stahl, 1958, using CsCl density gradient centrifugation).

**Q47. (MCQ)** A stretch of euchromatin has 200 nucleosomes. Number of base pairs? (one nucleosome ≈ 200 bp) *(SQP 26-27; similar 2025)*
**Answer:** 200 × 200 = **40,000 bp** (MS option C: 40,000 bp and 13,600 × 10⁻⁹ m, using 0.34 nm per bp).

**Q48. (3 marks)** Describe the nucleosome and differentiate euchromatin and heterochromatin. *(2025; similar 2016 D, 2017 OD)*
**Answer:** Histones are positively charged proteins (rich in lysine and arginine). 8 histone molecules form a **histone octamer**. Negatively charged DNA wraps around it → **nucleosome** (about 200 bp of DNA). Nucleosomes look like "beads-on-string" under the electron microscope and coil further into chromatin fibres (non-histone chromosomal (NHC) proteins help). **Euchromatin** = loosely packed, stains light, **transcriptionally active**. **Heterochromatin** = densely packed, stains dark, inactive.

**Q49. (3 marks)** Explain Hershey and Chase's experiment. *(2015 D, 2019 D; MCQ 2023 OD)*
**Answer:** They used bacteriophages. Some phages were grown with **radioactive phosphorus ³²P** (labels DNA), others with **radioactive sulphur ³⁵S** (labels protein). (1) **Infection:** phages infected E. coli. (2) **Blending:** a blender removed the phage coats from bacteria. (3) **Centrifugation:** bacteria settled at the bottom; phage coats stayed in the supernatant. Bacteria infected with ³²P phage were radioactive; with ³⁵S phage were not. So **DNA, not protein, enters the bacteria — DNA is the genetic material**.

**Q50. (2 marks)** Why is DNA a better genetic material than RNA? *(2020 OD; A-R 2024, 2023 OD)*
**Answer:** RNA has a reactive **2'-OH** group in every nucleotide, so it is labile, easily degraded and mutates fast (RNA viruses evolve fast). DNA is chemically more stable (no 2'-OH; thymine instead of uracil), so it is better for storing genetic information. RNA is better for expressing characters (protein synthesis).

**Q51. (3 marks)** Draw a replication fork and explain why replication occurs within a fork. Role of DNA ligase? *(2026; similar 2019 OD, 2019 D, 2016 D)*
**Answer:** (Diagram in Section 6.) The two DNA strands cannot be separated along the whole length at once because it needs very high energy, so replication happens in a small opening of the helix = **replication fork**. DNA polymerase works only in the **5'→3' direction**, so the template with 3'→5' polarity gives **continuous** synthesis (leading strand), and the template with 5'→3' polarity gives **discontinuous** synthesis (lagging strand, short fragments). **DNA ligase** joins these discontinuous fragments.

**Q52. (2 marks)** Draw/describe a transcription unit. Where are the promoter and terminator? *(2019 D, 2019 OD; MCQ 2024, SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** Transcription unit = **promoter + structural gene + terminator**. The promoter is towards the **5' end (upstream)** of the structural gene (with reference to the coding strand); the terminator is towards the **3' end (downstream)** of the coding strand. The template strand has 3'→5' polarity; the coding strand has 5'→3' polarity and the same sequence as RNA (T in place of U). In bacteria, RNA polymerase uses the **sigma factor** to start and the **rho factor** to stop.

**Q53. (3 marks)** Why must hnRNA be processed? Name the processes. Why is only one DNA strand transcribed? *(2026; similar 2017 D, 2023 OD)*
**Answer:** In eukaryotes the primary transcript (hnRNA) has both **exons** (coding) and **introns** (non-coding). (1) **Splicing** removes introns and joins exons. (2) **Capping**: methyl guanosine triphosphate is added at the 5' end. (3) **Tailing**: 200–300 adenylate residues (poly-A tail) are added at the 3' end. Then it is mRNA and goes to the cytoplasm. Only one strand is copied because (a) if both were copied, the two RNAs would be complementary and pair to form double-stranded RNA, so no protein would be made; (b) two different RNAs/proteins from the same DNA segment would make genetic information transfer complicated.

**Q54. (2–3 marks)** Name the three RNA polymerases in eukaryotes and what each makes. *(2023 OD)*
**Answer:** RNA polymerase I → rRNAs (28S, 18S, 5.8S); RNA polymerase II → hnRNA (precursor of mRNA); RNA polymerase III → tRNA, 5S rRNA and snRNAs.

**Q55. (2 marks)** When is the genetic code said to be (a) degenerate (b) universal? Also unambiguous? *(2020 D; similar 2016 OD, 2017 D, A-R 2025)*
**Answer:** (a) **Degenerate:** some amino acids are coded by more than one codon. (b) **Universal:** the same codon codes for the same amino acid in all organisms, from bacteria to humans (some exceptions in mitochondria and a few protozoans). **Unambiguous/specific:** one codon codes for only one amino acid. Other features: triplet; 61 codons for amino acids; 3 stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA); AUG has a dual role (codes methionine and acts as the start codon); read without commas.

**Q56. (3 marks)** Describe tRNA. Why is it called an adapter molecule? *(2017 D; similar 2024, 2023 OD)*
**Answer:** tRNA looks like a **clover leaf** (2-D); in 3-D it looks like an inverted L. It has an **anticodon loop** (bases complementary to the codon on mRNA) and an **amino acid acceptor end** (3' end) where a specific amino acid attaches (charging/aminoacylation, which uses ATP). It "reads" the code on mRNA and also carries the matching amino acid, so it is an **adapter** between nucleic acid and protein. Translation stops when a release factor binds the stop codon. UTRs (untranslated regions) are on mRNA at both the 5' end (before the start codon) and the 3' end (after the stop codon); they are needed for efficient translation.

**Q57. (5 marks)** Explain the lac operon (with inducer and without inducer). *(2024 OR, 2025 OR)*
**Answer:** The lac operon of E. coli has a regulator gene **i** (makes repressor), a **promoter (p)**, an **operator (o)** and 3 structural genes: **z** (β-galactosidase), **y** (permease), **a** (transacetylase).
- **No lactose (no inducer):** the repressor made by the i gene binds to the **operator** → RNA polymerase cannot transcribe → genes switched **off**.
- **Lactose present (inducer):** lactose (allolactose) binds the repressor and inactivates it → repressor cannot bind the operator → RNA polymerase transcribes z, y, a → enzymes made → lactose is used. Genes switched **on**.
- It is called **negative regulation** because the operon is controlled by a repressor.

**Q58. (3 marks)** Give four goals of the Human Genome Project (HGP). Name one non-human organism sequenced. *(2023 D; similar 2020 D, 2017 D)*
**Answer:** HGP began in 1990 and was completed in 2003. Goals (any four): identify all the ~20,000–25,000 genes in human DNA; find the sequence of the ~3 billion base pairs; store this information in databases; improve tools for data analysis; transfer related technologies to other sectors such as industries; address the ethical, legal and social issues (ELSI). Non-human organisms sequenced: bacteria, yeast, *Caenorhabditis elegans*, *Drosophila*, rice, *Arabidopsis* (any one).

**Q59. (5 marks)** Describe DNA fingerprinting steps and uses. *(2023 D; similar 2015 D, 2017 OD, 2018 OD, 2024 OR)*
**Answer:** Technique (Alec Jeffreys) uses **VNTRs** (Variable Number of Tandem Repeats — a satellite DNA that differs from person to person) as probe. Steps: (1) isolate DNA; (2) digest DNA with **restriction endonucleases**; (3) separate fragments by **gel electrophoresis**; (4) transfer (blot) fragments to nitrocellulose/nylon membrane; (5) **hybridise** with a labelled VNTR probe; (6) detect bands by **autoradiography**. Uses: forensic science (identify criminals, identify dead bodies after accidents), **paternity disputes**, studying population and genetic diversity.

### Chapter 6 — Evolution

**Q60. (MCQ)** Select the odd one in the context of convergent evolution: (A) eyes of octopus and mammals (B) flippers of penguins and dolphins (C) flying squirrel and flying phalanger (D) thorns of Bougainvillea and tendrils of Cucurbita. *(2026)*
**Answer:** (D) — these are **homologous** (divergent evolution); the others are analogous/convergent.

**Q61. (MCQ)** If the frequency of allele A is 0.6 in a population in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, the frequency of Aa is: (A) 0.21 (B) 0.42 (C) 0.48 (D) 0.32. *(2024)*
**Answer:** (C) 0.48. Steps: p = 0.6, q = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4, Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.48.

**Q62. (2–3 marks)** Explain p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Which factors disturb Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium? *(2015 OD, 2017 OD, 2018 OD; similar 2020 OD)*
**Answer:** Hardy-Weinberg principle: allele frequencies in a population stay constant from generation to generation (genetic equilibrium) if there is no disturbance. p = frequency of dominant allele A, q = frequency of recessive allele a, p + q = 1. Then p² = frequency of AA, 2pq = Aa, q² = aa, and p² + 2pq + q² = 1. Five factors change it: **gene migration (gene flow), genetic drift, mutation, genetic recombination, natural selection.**

**Q63. (2 marks)** Differentiate homologous and analogous organs with examples. *(2016 OD, 2018 OD; similar 2015 OD, 2020 OD, 2024)*
**Answer:** **Homologous:** same basic structure and origin, different functions → **divergent evolution** (forelimbs of whale, bat, cheetah and human; thorn of Bougainvillea and tendril of Cucurbita; vertebrate hearts/brains). **Analogous:** different structure/origin, same function → **convergent evolution** (wings of butterfly and bird; eyes of octopus and mammals; flippers of penguin and dolphin; sweet potato (root) and potato (stem)).

**Q64. (3 marks)** Explain industrial melanism as an example of natural selection. *(2023 D; similar 2015 D, 2017 OD)*
**Answer:** In England, before industrialisation (before 1850), tree trunks were covered by white lichens, so **white-winged moths** were hidden from predator birds and were more in number. After industrialisation, smoke and soot darkened the trunks and killed the lichens. Now **dark (melanic) moths** were hidden and survived, while white moths were eaten → dark moths became more common. Nature selected the better-adapted (fitter) form → natural selection.

**Q65. (2 marks)** Explain adaptive radiation with an example. *(2015 D; similar SQP 26-27 continental drift)*
**Answer:** Evolution of different species from one ancestor, spreading into different habitats in one geographical area. Examples: **Darwin's finches** of the Galapagos (different beak types); **Australian marsupials**, which evolved from one ancestral stock within the island continent. (SQP: due to continental drift, South American mammals were overridden by North American fauna when the continents joined; Australian marsupials survived because there was no competition from other mammals.)

**Q66. (2 marks)** Write the order of human evolution and one feature each. *(2025 MCQ, 2023 D MCQ, 2019 OD, 2017 D)*
**Answer:** Dryopithecus/Ramapithecus (~15 mya, ape-like) → Australopithecines (~2 mya, East African grasslands, stone weapons, ate fruit) → *Homo habilis* (first human-like, brain 650–800 cc, probably did not eat meat) → *Homo erectus* (~1.5 mya, brain ~900 cc, probably ate meat) → Neanderthal man (brain ~1400 cc, used hides to protect the body and buried the dead) → *Homo sapiens*.

**Answer:** (A) Female *Aedes* mosquito (also spreads chikungunya).

**Q68. (3 marks)** "Plasmodium needs both mosquito and human hosts." Explain its life cycle. *(2023 D; similar 2023 OD, 2025)*
**Answer:** Infected **female Anopheles** bites a human and injects **sporozoites** (infective stage). In the human: sporozoites multiply in **liver cells**, then attack **red blood cells**, multiply and burst them, releasing **haemozoin** — a toxin causing chill and high fever every 3–4 days. Some parasites form **gametocytes** in RBCs. When a female Anopheles sucks this blood, gametocytes enter it; **fertilisation and development** happen in the **mosquito's stomach (gut)**; sporozoites form and are stored in the mosquito's **salivary glands**. So the asexual phase is in humans and the sexual phase in the mosquito.

**Q70. (3 marks)** Differentiate active and passive immunity; humoral and cell-mediated immunity. *(2026, 2024; similar 2019 OD, 2022)*
**Answer:**
- **Active immunity:** the body itself makes antibodies after meeting an antigen (infection or vaccine); slow to develop, long-lasting.
- **Passive immunity:** ready-made antibodies are given (colostrum IgA from mother; antitoxin for tetanus or snake bite); quick, short-lived.
- **Humoral immunity (antibody-mediated):** **B-lymphocytes** make antibodies that circulate in blood/lymph.
- **Cell-mediated immunity (CMI):** done by **T-lymphocytes**; responsible for **graft (organ transplant) rejection**. T-cells also help B-cells make antibodies.

**Q71. (2 marks)** Why does a vaccinated child not get the disease? Why don't people get measles/chickenpox twice? *(2020 OD, 2016 OD, 2019 D)*
**Answer:** The vaccine (weakened/killed pathogen or its proteins) or first infection produces a **primary response** and also **memory B and T cells**. On the next exposure, these memory cells quickly produce a massive **secondary (anamnestic) response** that destroys the pathogen before disease develops.

**Q72. (2 marks)** Explain cytokine barriers and the role of the spleen. *(2018 OD, 2025; spleen 2026)*
**Answer:** **Cytokine barrier:** virus-infected cells secrete proteins called **interferons**, which protect non-infected cells from further viral infection. **Spleen:** a large bean-shaped secondary lymphoid organ with lymphocytes and phagocytes; it filters blood by trapping blood-borne microorganisms and is also a large reservoir of erythrocytes.

**Q73. (4 marks — case)** Which cells does HIV attack? What happens inside? Test used? *(2024 case; similar 2019 D, 2020 OD, 2020 D)*
**Answer:** HIV (a retrovirus with RNA) first enters **macrophages**: the enzyme **reverse transcriptase** makes viral DNA from viral RNA; viral DNA joins the host cell's DNA and the infected cell makes new viruses (macrophage becomes an "HIV factory"). HIV then enters **helper T-lymphocytes (TH cells)**, multiplies and kills them, so the number of helper T cells falls → immunodeficiency → the patient dies from other (opportunistic) infections such as bacteria, viruses, fungi and *Toxoplasma*. Test: **ELISA (Enzyme Linked Immuno-Sorbent Assay)**. Treatment: anti-retroviral drugs, only partly effective (prolong life). Prevention: screen blood, use disposable needles and syringes, safe sex/condoms, awareness (NACO).

**Q74. (3 marks)** Differentiate addiction and dependence. Why does a user increase the dose over time? Also name the source of heroin. *(SQP 26-27; similar 2018 OD, 2020 D, 2024, 2025)*
**Answer:** **Addiction** = psychological attachment to the "high"/euphoria of a drug, even when it is not needed. **Dependence** = the body's physical need for the drug; stopping it causes **withdrawal symptoms** (anxiety, shakiness, nausea, sweating). Repeated use raises the **tolerance** of body receptors, so they respond only to higher doses. Heroin ("smack", diacetylmorphine) is made by **acetylation of morphine**, which comes from the latex of the poppy *Papaver somniferum*; it is a depressant. Cannabinoids (*Cannabis sativa*) affect the cardiovascular system; cocaine (*Erythroxylum coca*) interferes with dopamine transport and is a stimulant.

**Q76. (3 marks)** Explain secondary (biological) treatment of sewage. What is BOD? *(2017 D; similar 2015 OD, 2016 D, 2017 OD, 2019 OD, 2022)*
**Answer:** Primary effluent goes into large **aeration tanks**, where it is constantly agitated mechanically and air is pumped in → useful aerobic microbes grow into **flocs** (masses of bacteria with fungal filaments). They consume most of the organic matter, so **BOD falls**. The effluent then goes to a **settling tank**, where flocs settle = **activated sludge**. A small part is pumped back to the aeration tank as **inoculum**; the major part goes to **anaerobic sludge digesters**, where anaerobic bacteria digest it and produce **biogas** (methane, hydrogen sulphide, CO₂). The treated effluent is released into rivers. **BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)** = the amount of oxygen that would be consumed if all the organic matter in one litre of water were oxidised by bacteria; higher BOD = more polluted water. (SQP A-R: the aim of sewage treatment is to reduce organic matter; high BOD means **more**, not less, organic matter.)

**Q77. (2 marks)** Biogas: which microbes, where found, which institutes developed the technology? *(2023 OD; similar 2015 OD, 2019 OD, 2022)*
**Answer:** **Methanogens** (e.g., *Methanobacterium*) grow anaerobically on cellulose and produce methane with CO₂ and H₂. Found in anaerobic sludge during sewage treatment and in the rumen of cattle (so cattle dung, "gobar", is used). The technology was developed in India mainly by **IARI (Indian Agricultural Research Institute)** and **KVIC (Khadi and Village Industries Commission)**.

**Q78. (3 marks)** Why are baculoviruses (Nucleopolyhedrovirus) good biocontrol agents? Name other biocontrol agents and biofertilisers. *(SQP 26-27 case; similar 2016 OD, 2020 OD, 2022, 2023 D)*
**Answer:** Baculoviruses (genus **Nucleopolyhedrovirus**) attack insects and arthropods. They are **species-specific, narrow-spectrum** insecticides with **no negative effect** on plants, mammals, birds, fish or non-target insects — so they are ideal for **Integrated Pest Management (IPM)** and ecologically sensitive areas. Other biocontrol agents: **ladybird** (eats aphids), **dragonflies** (mosquitoes), ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** (spores sprayed on plants kill butterfly caterpillars — toxin is released in the larval gut), ***Trichoderma*** (free-living fungus in root ecosystems, controls plant pathogens). Biofertilisers: *Rhizobium* (root nodules of legumes, fixes N₂), *Azospirillum* and *Azotobacter* (free-living N₂ fixers), **mycorrhiza** such as ***Glomus*** (absorbs **phosphorus** from soil, gives resistance to root pathogens, tolerance to salinity and drought — SQP Q12), cyanobacteria *Anabaena*, *Nostoc*, *Oscillatoria* (fix N₂ in paddy fields, add organic matter).

### Chapters 9 & 10 — Biotechnology

**Q79. (MCQ)** Which is NOT a feature of plasmids? (A) circular (B) self-replicating (C) single-stranded (D) extra-chromosomal. *(2024)*
**Answer:** (C) Plasmids are double-stranded.

**Q80. (Assertion–Reason)** A: Chitinase is needed to separate DNA from yeast cells but not from *Spirogyra*. R: Fungal cell wall is made of chitin. *(2026; similar 2024, SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** (A) Both true, R explains A. (Use **lysozyme** for bacteria, **cellulase** for plant/algal cells, **chitinase** for fungi.)

**Q81. (3 marks)** Explain naming of restriction enzymes and formation of recombinant DNA using EcoRI. *(2019 D; similar 2017 D, 2019 OD, 2020 OD, 2025)*
**Answer:** **EcoRI:** E = genus *Escherichia*, co = species *coli*, R = strain RY13, I = first enzyme isolated from this strain. Restriction endonucleases recognise a **palindromic sequence** (reads the same on both strands in the 5'→3' direction), e.g. EcoRI site:
5'–GAATTC–3'
3'–CTTAAG–5'
EcoRI cuts between G and A on both strands, a little away from the centre, leaving single-stranded overhangs = **sticky ends**. If the vector and the foreign DNA are cut with the **same enzyme**, they have matching sticky ends, which pair by hydrogen bonds; **DNA ligase** then seals them → **recombinant DNA**.

**Q82. (3 marks)** In pBR322, state the role of ori, ampR/tetR, rop and restriction sites. Explain insertional inactivation. *(2020 OD, 2024, 2026 case; similar 2016 OD, 2019 D, 2023 OD, SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** **ori** — origin of replication; any DNA linked to it can replicate in the host and it controls the **copy number**. **ampR and tetR** — antibiotic-resistance genes (ampicillin, tetracycline) used as **selectable markers** to identify transformants. **rop** — codes for proteins involved in replication of the plasmid. **Restriction sites** — single sites (EcoRI, HindIII, ClaI, PvuII, BamHI, SalI, PstI, PvuI) where foreign DNA can be inserted. (BamHI and SalI are in tetR; PstI and PvuI in ampR; PvuII in rop.) **Insertional inactivation:** foreign DNA is inserted into one marker gene, e.g., tetR at BamHI — that gene stops working. Recombinants grow on ampicillin but **not** on tetracycline; non-recombinants grow on both. Easier method: insert the foreign DNA into the **β-galactosidase** gene — recombinants give **colourless/white** colonies; non-recombinants give **blue** colonies on a chromogenic substrate.

**Q83. (2 marks)** How are bacteria made competent? Name two vector-less methods of gene transfer. *(2026, 2024; similar 2016 OD, 2025)*
**Answer:** DNA is hydrophilic and cannot pass cell membranes. Bacterial cells are treated with a specific concentration of a divalent cation, **calcium (Ca²⁺)**, then placed on **ice**, given a **heat shock (42°C)** and put back on ice → recombinant DNA enters. Vector-less methods: **microinjection** (DNA injected directly into the nucleus of an animal cell); **biolistics/gene gun** (plant cells bombarded with high-velocity gold or tungsten micro-particles coated with DNA).

**Q84. (3 marks)** Explain gel electrophoresis. *(2025; similar 2016 D, 2017 OD, 2024)*
**Answer:** DNA fragments are **negatively charged**, so in an electric field they move towards the **anode** through **agarose gel** (from seaweed). The gel acts as a sieve: **smaller fragments move farther**. Separated DNA is stained with **ethidium bromide** and seen as bright **orange bands under UV light**. The bands are cut out and the DNA extracted (**elution**), then used to make recombinant DNA by joining with cloning vectors, in DNA fingerprinting, etc.

**Q85. (3 marks)** Describe PCR. Role of Taq polymerase? *(2017 D; similar 2015 OD, 2016 D, 2019 OD, 2023 D, 2023 OD)*
**Answer:** PCR = Polymerase Chain Reaction, used to make many copies of a gene in vitro. Each cycle: (1) **Denaturation** — high temperature separates the two DNA strands. (2) **Annealing** — two sets of **primers** (small, chemically synthesised oligonucleotides) bind to complementary regions. (3) **Extension** — DNA polymerase extends the primers using nucleotides. Repeating ~30 times amplifies the DNA about **1 billion times**. **Taq polymerase** is a **thermostable** DNA polymerase from the bacterium ***Thermus aquaticus***; it stays active at the high temperature of denaturation, so new enzyme need not be added in every cycle.

**Q86. (2 marks)** Why are bioreactors used? Describe a stirred-tank bioreactor. *(2018 OD; similar 2015 D, 2019 OD, 2023 D, 2025)*
**Answer:** Bioreactors (100–1000 litres) allow large-scale production of products by providing optimum growth conditions (temperature, pH, substrate, salts, vitamins, oxygen). **Stirred-tank** bioreactor: usually cylindrical with a curved base for mixing; a **stirrer** mixes contents evenly and increases oxygen availability; in the **sparged** type, sterile air is bubbled in. It also has a foam-control system, temperature and pH control, and sampling ports. (SQP: a fall in yield due to contamination is prevented by **sterile handling, sterilised equipment and aseptic transfer**.)

**Q87. (3 marks)** How was human insulin made by rDNA technology? *(2015 OD, 2015 D, 2017 D; similar 2018 OD, 2020 OD)*
**Answer:** Insulin has two short chains, **A and B**, joined by **disulphide bridges**. In the body it is made as **pro-insulin**, which has an extra stretch, the **C-peptide**, that is removed to make mature insulin. In 1983 **Eli Lilly** prepared two DNA sequences for chains A and B, introduced them into **plasmids of E. coli**, produced the chains separately, extracted them and combined them by creating disulphide bonds → human insulin (**Humulin**). It avoids allergy/reactions caused by insulin from cattle or pigs.

**Q88. (2–3 marks)** Explain gene therapy for ADA deficiency. Why is periodic infusion needed? *(2025; similar 2015 OD, 2016 OD, 2019 OD)*
**Answer:** **Adenosine deaminase (ADA)** is needed for the immune system; its deficiency (deletion of the ADA gene) causes immunodeficiency. In 1990 a 4-year-old girl was treated: her **lymphocytes** were grown in culture, a functional **ADA cDNA** was introduced into them using a **retroviral vector**, and the cells were returned to her body. These lymphocytes are **not immortal**, so periodic infusion is needed. Permanent cure: introduce the ADA gene isolated from bone-marrow cells into cells at **early embryonic stages**. (Other options: bone-marrow transplant, enzyme replacement therapy — injecting functional ADA.)

**Q89. (3 marks)** How was Bt cotton made pest resistant? Why does the Bt toxin not kill the bacterium? *(2023 D; similar 2017 D, 2017 OD, 2020 D, 2020 OD, 2022, 2024 OR)*
**Answer:** *Bacillus thuringiensis* makes **Bt toxin** protein crystals that kill certain insects (lepidopterans like bollworms, coleopterans, dipterans). The toxin exists as an **inactive protoxin**, so the bacterium is not harmed. When an insect eats it, the **alkaline pH of the insect gut** dissolves the crystals and activates the toxin; the active toxin binds to the surface of **midgut epithelial cells**, makes **pores**, cells swell and burst (lyse) → insect dies. The toxin is coded by ***cry*** genes; **cryIAc and cryIIAb** control cotton bollworms, **cryIAb** controls corn borer. These genes were introduced into cotton → Bt cotton.

**Q90. (3 marks)** How was tobacco made resistant to the nematode *Meloidegyne incognitia*? *(2024; similar 2016 D, 2018 OD, 2023 OD, MCQ 2025, SQP 26-27)*
**Answer:** The nematode infects tobacco **roots** and reduces yield. Using ***Agrobacterium*** vectors, nematode-specific genes were introduced into tobacco so that the plant made both **sense and anti-sense RNA**. These two complementary RNAs formed **double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)**, which started **RNA interference (RNAi)**: it **silenced the specific mRNA of the nematode** (stopped its translation). So the parasite could not survive in the transgenic plant.

**Q91. (3 marks)** List benefits of GM plants. Give uses of transgenic animals. *(2016 D, 2019 OD, 2020 D, 2026; similar 2015 OD)*
**Answer:** GM plants: (1) more tolerant to abiotic stresses (cold, drought, salt, heat); (2) less use of chemical pesticides (pest-resistant crops); (3) fewer post-harvest losses; (4) better use of minerals by plants (prevents early exhaustion of soil fertility); (5) more nutritional value — e.g., **Golden rice**, rich in vitamin A. Transgenic animals: study of normal physiology and development; study of diseases; biological products (e.g., **α-1-antitrypsin** for emphysema; transgenic cow **Rosie** (1997) gave human-protein-enriched milk, 2.4 g/L α-lactalbumin); vaccine safety testing (e.g., polio vaccine on transgenic mice); chemical safety (toxicity) testing.

**Q92. (1–2 marks)** What is biopiracy? What is GEAC? *(2015 D, 2016 OD, 2017 OD; MCQ 2025)*
**Answer:** **Biopiracy** = use of bio-resources (and traditional knowledge) of a country by multinational companies or other organisations without proper authorisation from, or compensation to, the countries and people concerned (e.g., an American company got a patent on Basmati rice in 1997). **GEAC = Genetic Engineering Approval Committee** (Indian government body that decides on the validity of GM research and the safety of introducing GM organisms for public use).

**Q93. (MCQ)** An interaction where one species benefits and the other is neither harmed nor benefited: (A) mycorrhiza (B) sea anemone and clownfish (C) Abingdon tortoise and goats (D) Cuscuta on hedge plants. *(2026)*
**Answer:** (B) Commensalism.

**Q94. (3 marks — learn as a table)** Population interactions with examples. *(2016 OD, 2019 OD, 2023 D, 2023 OD, 2024, 2026; SQP 26-27)*

| Interaction | Sign | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Mutualism | + / + | lichen (fungus + alga); mycorrhiza (fungus + plant roots); fig tree and wasp; orchid *Ophrys* and bee (pseudocopulation); plant and pollinator |
| Competition | − / − | Abingdon tortoise and goats in Galapagos (tortoises became extinct) |
| Predation | + / − | tiger–deer; Monarch butterfly is distasteful to predators due to a chemical it gets as a caterpillar by feeding on a poisonous weed |
| Parasitism | + / − | *Cuscuta* on hedge plants; **brood parasitism** — cuckoo (koel) lays eggs in a crow's nest; eggs look similar, so the crow incubates them |

**Gause's competitive exclusion principle (2026):** two closely related species competing for the same limited resources cannot coexist indefinitely; the competitively inferior one is eliminated. (MacArthur's warblers coexist by **resource partitioning** — feeding in different parts of the tree.)
**Plant defence (2023 D):** cattle and goats do not browse *Calotropis* because it makes highly poisonous **cardiac glycosides**. Other plants have thorns (Acacia, cactus) as mechanical defence.
**Positive roles of predators (2024):** pass energy across trophic levels; keep prey populations under control (prickly pear cactus in Australia was controlled by a cactus-feeding moth); maintain species diversity by reducing competition among prey (removing the starfish *Pisaster* led to extinction of more than 10 invertebrate species).

**Q95. (3 marks)** Name population attributes, draw age pyramids and give growth equations. *(2017 OD, 2020 OD, 2022, 2025; similar 2015 OD, 2016 OD, 2017 D, 2019 D)*
**Answer:** Attributes of a population (not of an individual): **birth rate (natality), death rate (mortality), sex ratio, age distribution**. Age pyramids: **expanding** (triangular — many young, growing population), **stable** (bell-shaped), **declining** (urn-shaped — fewer young). Density measured by counting, per cent cover, **pug marks and fecal pellets** (tigers), catch per trap (fish). Growth: Nₜ₊₁ = Nₜ + [(B + I) − (D + E)]. Exponential growth dN/dt = rN gives a **J-shaped** curve (unlimited resources). Logistic growth dN/dt = rN[(K − N)/K] gives an **S-shaped (sigmoid)** curve; **K = carrying capacity**. r = intrinsic rate of natural increase.
Example (2025): if density at t + 1 = 800, natality 200, immigration 200, mortality 150, emigration 100 → 800 = Nₜ + (200 + 200) − (150 + 100) → **Nₜ = 650**.
Example (2019 D): 100 frogs, 20 born in a year → birth rate = 20/100 = **0.2 offspring per frog per year**.

### Chapter 12 — Ecosystem

**Q96. (2 marks)** Differentiate GPP and NPP. Calculate NPP when GPP = 3500 and R = 1300 g/m²/year. *(SQP 26-27; similar 2025, 2017 D, 2015 D)*
**Answer:** **GPP (gross primary productivity)** = rate of production of organic matter in photosynthesis. **NPP** = GPP − R (respiration losses); it is the biomass available to consumers (herbivores and decomposers). NPP = 3500 − 1300 = **2200 g/m²/year**. Productivity is expressed in g m⁻² yr⁻¹ or (kcal m⁻²) yr⁻¹.

**Q97. (5 marks)** Explain the steps of decomposition and factors affecting it. *(SQP 26-27 OR; similar 2020 D, 2024, A-R 2023 D, MCQ 2025)*
**Answer:** (1) **Fragmentation** — detritivores (earthworm, termites) break detritus into small particles. (2) **Leaching** — water-soluble inorganic nutrients go down into the soil and get precipitated as unavailable salts. (3) **Catabolism** — bacterial and fungal enzymes break detritus into simple inorganic substances. (4) **Humification** — forms **humus**, dark amorphous material, highly resistant to microbial action, decomposes very slowly; it is a reservoir of nutrients. (5) **Mineralisation** — humus is further broken by microbes to release inorganic nutrients. Factors: decomposition is **slow** if detritus is rich in **lignin and chitin**, fast if rich in **nitrogen and water-soluble sugars**; **warm and moist** conditions speed it up; low temperature, dryness and anaerobic conditions slow it.

**Q98. (2–3 marks)** Differentiate grazing and detritus food chains. Why is the pyramid of biomass in the sea inverted? Limitations of ecological pyramids? *(2026; MCQ 2024)*
**Answer:** **GFC** starts from green plants (producers), energy source is the sun (grass → goat → man). **DFC** starts from dead organic matter, with decomposers (fungi, bacteria — saprotrophs). In aquatic ecosystems GFC carries more energy; in terrestrial ecosystems more energy flows through DFC. Sea: biomass of fishes is **more** than that of phytoplankton (which reproduce fast but are small) → **inverted** biomass pyramid. The pyramid of **energy is always upright**. Limitations: (1) does not consider the same species at two or more trophic levels; (2) assumes a simple food chain, not a food web; (3) saprophytes/decomposers are not given any place.

### Chapter 13 — Biodiversity and its Conservation

**Q99. (MCQ)** Which fish led to the extinction of more than 200 species of cichlid fish in Lake Victoria? (A) Catla (B) Dogfish (C) Nile perch (D) African catfish. *(2026)*
**Answer:** (C) Nile perch.

**Q100. (3 marks)** Explain the three levels of biodiversity with examples. *(2026; similar 2016 D, 2018 OD)*
**Answer:** **Genetic diversity** — variation within a species: *Rauwolfia vomitoria* in the Himalaya differs in potency/concentration of reserpine; India has more than 50,000 strains of rice and 1,000 varieties of mango. **Species diversity** — variety of species: the Western Ghats have more amphibian species than the Eastern Ghats. **Ecological diversity** — variety of ecosystems: India has deserts, rainforests, mangroves, coral reefs, wetlands, estuaries and alpine meadows, so it has more ecosystem diversity than Norway.

**Q101. (4–5 marks)** Explain the causes of biodiversity loss ("Evil Quartet"). *(SQP 26-27; similar 2017 OD, 2019 D, 2020 OD, 2025)*
**Answer:**
1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation** — most important cause; e.g., tropical rainforests once covered 14% of Earth's land, now only 6%; the Amazon is being cleared for soybean and cattle ranches. Fragmentation hurts animals that need large territories and migratory animals.
2. **Over-exploitation** — e.g., Steller's sea cow and passenger pigeon became extinct due to over-exploitation by humans; many marine fish are over-harvested.
3. **Alien species invasions** — Nile perch in Lake Victoria (200+ cichlid species lost); carrot grass (*Parthenium*), *Lantana* and water hyacinth (*Eichhornia*); African catfish *Clarias gariepinus* threatens native catfishes in Indian rivers.
4. **Co-extinctions** — when a species becomes extinct, plants and animals obligately linked to it also become extinct (e.g., a host fish and its parasites; plant–pollinator mutualism).
IUCN Red List (2004): **784 species** became extinct in the last 500 years (**338 vertebrates, 359 invertebrates, 87 plants**). Recent extinctions: dodo (Mauritius), quagga (Africa), thylacine (Australia), Steller's sea cow (Russia), three subspecies of tiger (Bali, Javan, Caspian).

**Q102. (3–5 marks)** Explain in-situ and ex-situ conservation. What are hotspots and sacred groves? *(2016 D, 2017 OD, 2019 OD, 2025; similar 2015 OD, 2019 D, MCQ 2023 OD)*
**Answer:** **In-situ (on site):** protect species in their natural habitat — **biodiversity hotspots** (regions with very high species richness and high **endemism**; 34 in the world; India has 3 that also cover neighbouring countries: **Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Indo-Burma, Himalaya**; hotspots cover less than 2% of the earth's land area), protected areas like **biosphere reserves, national parks and wildlife sanctuaries**, and **sacred groves** (forest patches protected by tribal communities for religious reasons — Khasi and Jaintia Hills in Meghalaya, Aravalli Hills of Rajasthan, Western Ghats of Karnataka and Maharashtra, Sarguja, Chanda and Bastar areas of MP/Chhattisgarh). Note: Jaintia Hills are a sacred grove area, **not** a hotspot (2023 OD MCQ). **Ex-situ (off site):** threatened species taken out of the habitat and protected in **zoological parks, botanical gardens, wildlife safari parks**, and by **cryopreservation** of gametes, in vitro fertilisation, tissue culture, and **seed banks**. **IUCN Red Data List** records threatened and extinct species and helps decide conservation priorities.

**Q103. (3 marks)** Why should we conserve biodiversity? *(2023 D; similar 2016 D)*
**Answer:** (1) **Narrowly utilitarian:** humans get food (cereals, pulses, fruits), firewood, fibre, construction material, industrial products (tannins, lubricants, dyes, resins, perfumes) and medicines (more than 25% of drugs sold come from plants); **bioprospecting** looks for useful products. (2) **Broadly utilitarian:** ecosystem services — the Amazon forest produces about 20% of the Earth's oxygen through photosynthesis; pollination by bees, birds and bats; aesthetic pleasure. (3) **Ethical:** every species has an intrinsic value; we have a moral duty to care for them and pass them on to future generations.

### 6.4 Practical viva quick answers
- **Pollen germination:** sucrose (or sugar) + boric acid solution; pollen tube grows in a few minutes; seen under microscope.
- **Onion root tip mitosis:** fix in Carnoy's/acetic-alcohol, hydrolyse with dilute HCl (to soften), stain with **acetocarmine**; stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase; root tip is used because it is a meristem (cells divide actively).
- **Quadrat method:** density = total number of individuals ÷ number of quadrats studied; frequency (%) = (number of quadrats in which the species occurs ÷ total quadrats) × 100.
- **DNA isolation from spinach/banana/peas:** crush with salt + detergent (breaks membranes) → filter → add enzyme (meat tenderiser = protease) → add **chilled ethanol** → DNA appears as white threads, spooled on a glass rod.
- **Emasculation, bagging, tagging** — see Q9. Tagging = label with date and parents' names.

## 7. Answer-writing tips and 30-day timetable

### 7.1 How to grab step marks
1. **Write the keyword.** Marking schemes give marks for key words: "filiform apparatus", "triple fusion", "inactive protoxin", "alkaline pH", "sticky ends", "selectable marker", "insertional inactivation", "semi-conservative", "memory cells". Underline them.
2. **2-mark answers = 2 points; 3-mark answers = 3 points.** Number your points. Don't write a story.
3. **Draw whenever possible.** A labelled diagram often gets marks even if the explanation is weak. Even in a non-diagram question, a small sketch of a cross or a flow chart helps.
4. **Crosses:** always write Parents → Gametes → Punnett square → F1/F2 → Ratio (phenotype and genotype). Each step carries marks.
5. **Numericals (productivity, DNA bases, population):** write the formula first, then put values, then the answer with units.
6. **Differences:** use a 2-column table with the same "basis" in each row.
7. **Scientific names:** first letter capital for genus, underline (e.g., <u>Bacillus thuringiensis</u>). Spell them correctly — learn the 15 names in Q69, Q75, Q78.
8. **Assertion–Reason:** first decide if A is true, then if R is true, then check "because". If A is false, the answer is D (if R is true) — don't overthink.
9. **Case-based questions (Section D):** answers are usually in the passage or are basic NCERT facts. Never leave them blank.
10. **Attempt all 33 questions.** Start with Section A (16 marks). Then do the chapters you studied in Sections B–E. In internal choices, pick the option from your strong chapter.
11. **Time:** Section A — 20 min; B — 20 min; C — 45 min; D — 20 min; E — 45 min; revise — 15 min.
