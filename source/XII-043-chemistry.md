## 3. The 33-mark plan (target about 40% = 28 marks in theory)

**Main idea:** You do not need the whole book. Learn the **easy, repeated** parts of 6 chapters very well, and the named reactions from organic chemistry. Attempt **every** MCQ, because there is no negative marking.

### 3a. Study order (easiest and highest return first)

1. **Biomolecules (7)**: pure memory, with the same questions every year (glucose reactions, peptide/glycosidic linkage, DNA vs RNA, vitamins).
2. **Coordination Compounds (7)**: fixed rules (IUPAC naming, hybridisation, magnetic behaviour, Werner AgCl questions).
3. **Solutions (7)**: 4 formulas cover most numericals, plus azeotropes and positive/negative deviation.
4. **Chemical Kinetics (7)**: first-order formula, half-life, order from data, Arrhenius.
5. **Electrochemistry (9)**: the Nernst equation numerical comes **every year**, plus Kohlrausch, Faraday and batteries.
6. **Named reactions (organic, about 26 marks available)**: learn the 20 reactions in Section 6. Many 2- and 3-mark questions ask only "write the reaction" or "identify A and B".
7. **d- and f-Block (7)**: learn the 10 standard "give reasons" answers. This is a bonus chapter.

**MCQ section:** Of the 16 MCQs, about 10-11 come from the 6 priority chapters above. Aim for 9-10 correct out of 16.

### 3c. How practicals save you

- Practical is a separate pass (10/30 needed), but students usually score 22-28/30 there. **Do not lose these marks.**
- Get a complete, signed **practical file** (record + viva 4 marks) and a neat **project** (4 marks). That is 8 marks before the exam starts.
- Volumetric (8): learn the formula **a1 M1 V1 = a2 M2 V2** (KMnO4 a = 5; oxalic acid a = 2; Mohr's salt a = 1), and the end point: colourless to **light pink** (KMnO4 acts as its own indicator).
- Salt analysis (8): write the steps even if the final answer is wrong. Examiners give step marks for the preliminary test, dry test, wet test and confirmatory test.
- Viva favourites: why dilute H2SO4 (not HCl or HNO3) is used in KMnO4 titrations (HCl is oxidised by KMnO4; HNO3 is itself an oxidising agent); why oxalic acid is warmed to 60-70 °C; what Rf is (distance moved by substance / distance moved by solvent).

## 5. Must-do question bank (72 questions)

★ = asked many times, so do this first. Years in brackets are papers where the same or a very similar question appeared. "SQP" = 2026-27 Sample Paper.

### Chapter 1: Solutions

**Q1 ★ (MCQ, 2026)** Which solution will have the lowest freezing point? (A) 0.1 M glucose (B) 0.1 M CaCl2 (C) 0.1 M KCl (D) 0.1 M urea
**Ans: (B) CaCl2.** It gives 3 ions (i = 3), so it has the most particles, the largest ΔTf and the lowest freezing point.

**Q2 (MCQ, 2025)** Which aqueous solution has the highest freezing point? (A) 1.0 M KCl (B) 1.0 M Na2SO4 (C) 1.0 M glucose (D) 1.0 M AlCl3
**Ans: (C) glucose.** It is a non-electrolyte (i = 1), so it has the fewest particles and the smallest ΔTf.

**Q3 (MCQ, 2023)** The colligative property used to find the molar mass of polymers and proteins is: **Ans: osmotic pressure.** It is measured at room temperature and gives a measurable value even for very dilute solutions.

**Q4 ★ (A-R, 2026)** A: The components of an azeotrope cannot be separated by fractional distillation. R: Components of an azeotrope have the same composition in the liquid and vapour phase and boil at constant temperature.
**Ans: (A)** Both are true, and R explains A.

**Q5 (A-R, 2025)** A: Henry's law constant KH decreases with increase in temperature. R: As temperature increases, the solubility of gases in liquids decreases.
**Ans: (D)** A is false (KH **increases** with temperature), and R is true.

**Q6 ★ (2 marks, 2025)** Calculate the elevation in boiling point when 3 g CaCl2 (M = 111) is dissolved in 260 g water (complete dissociation, Kb = 0.52 K kg/mol).
**Ans:**
- Moles of CaCl2 = 3/111 = 0.0270 mol
- m = 0.0270 / 0.260 kg = 0.104 mol/kg
- CaCl2 → Ca2+ + 2Cl-, so i = 3
- ΔTb = i × Kb × m = 3 × 0.52 × 0.104 = **0.162 K**

**Q7 ★ (3 marks, 2026)** Calculate the boiling point of a solution of 0.61 g benzoic acid (M = 122) in 5 g CS2, if benzoic acid dimerises to the extent of 88%. Boiling point of CS2 = 46.2 °C, Kb = 2.3 K kg/mol.
**Ans:**
- For dimerisation (2A → A2): i = 1 - α/2 = 1 - 0.88/2 = **0.56**
- m = (0.61/122) / 0.005 kg = 0.005/0.005 = 1 mol/kg
- ΔTb = i Kb m = 0.56 × 2.3 × 1 = 1.288 K
- Boiling point = 46.2 + 1.288 = **47.49 °C**

**Q8 ★ (3 marks, 2016)** Calculate the freezing point of a solution of 1.9 g MgCl2 (M = 95) in 50 g water, assuming complete ionisation (Kf = 1.86 K kg/mol).
**Ans:** n = 1.9/95 = 0.02 mol; m = 0.02/0.05 = 0.4 mol/kg; i = 3. ΔTf = 3 × 1.86 × 0.4 = 2.23 K. Freezing point = 273.15 - 2.23 = **270.92 K**.

**Q9 ★ (3 marks, 2019; same idea 2017)** A 4% (w/w) solution of sucrose (M = 342) freezes at 271.15 K. Find the freezing point of a 5% glucose (M = 180) solution. (Pure water freezes at 273.15 K.)
**Ans:**
- Sucrose: ΔTf = 2 K. Here m = (4/342) / 0.096 kg = 0.1218 mol/kg.
- Glucose: m = (5/180) / 0.095 kg = 0.2924 mol/kg.
- Same Kf, so ΔTf(glucose) = 2 × (0.2924/0.1218) = 4.80 K
- Freezing point = 273.15 - 4.80 = **268.35 K**

**Q10 (3 marks, 2015)** 3.9 g benzoic acid in 49 g benzene lowers the freezing point by 1.62 K. Find i and the nature of the solute (M = 122, Kf = 4.9).
**Ans:** m = (3.9/122) / 0.049 = 0.652 mol/kg. Normal ΔTf = 4.9 × 0.652 = 3.20 K. i = 1.62/3.20 = **0.51**. Since i < 1, benzoic acid is **associated** (it forms a dimer).

**Q11 (2 marks, 2020)** Calculate the osmotic pressure of a 5% (w/v) urea solution (M = 60) at 300 K (R = 0.0821 L atm/K/mol).
**Ans:** 5 g in 100 mL, so C = (5/60)/0.1 L = 0.833 mol/L. π = CRT = 0.833 × 0.0821 × 300 = **20.5 atm**.

**Q12 (3 marks, 2023)** 30 g urea (M = 60) is dissolved in 846 g water. Find the vapour pressure of water if p° = 23.8 mm Hg.
**Ans:** n(urea) = 0.5 mol; n(water) = 846/18 = 47 mol. x(water) = 47/47.5 = 0.9895. p = x × p° = 0.9895 × 23.8 = **23.55 mm Hg**.

**Q13 (2 marks, 2025)** Liquids X and Y form an ideal solution. p°X = 120 mm Hg and p°Y = 160 mm Hg. Find the vapour pressure of a solution with equal moles of X and Y.
**Ans:** xX = xY = 0.5. p = 0.5 × 120 + 0.5 × 160 = **140 mm Hg**.

**Q14 ★ (2-3 marks, 2025, 2026, SQP)** A student mixes two liquids (10 mL + 10 mL) and the volume becomes 20.2 mL. (i) Why? (ii) Will the temperature rise or fall? (iii) Give an example. Also: what type of deviation does phenol + aniline show?
**Ans:**
- (i) The volume increases because A-B attractions are **weaker** than A-A and B-B attractions. This is a **positive deviation** from Raoult's law (ΔmixV > 0).
- (ii) Heat is absorbed (ΔmixH > 0), so the **temperature falls**.
- (iii) Examples: ethanol + acetone, or acetone + CS2.
- Phenol + aniline shows **negative deviation**. They form strong H-bonds (O-H...N), so the vapour pressure falls and the **boiling point rises**. Volume decreases and heat is released.

**Q15 (2 marks, 2025, 2016, 2024)** (i) Why is salt sprinkled on snowy roads? (ii) What happens when RBCs are put in 0.5% NaCl solution? (iii) Give one use of reverse osmosis.
**Ans:**
- (i) Salt lowers the freezing point of water, so the ice melts.
- (ii) 0.5% NaCl is **hypotonic** (blood is isotonic with 0.9% NaCl). Water enters the cells and they **swell and may burst**. In more than 0.9% NaCl (hypertonic), the cells shrink.
- (iii) **Desalination** of sea water.

**Q16 (1 mark, SQP, 2023)** Why do people at high altitude have low oxygen in their blood? **Ans:** The atmospheric pressure, and so the partial pressure of O2, is low. By Henry's law (p = KH × x), less O2 dissolves in blood (this is called anoxia).

### Chapter 2: Electrochemistry

**Q17 ★ (5 marks, 2026; 2025 similar)** Calculate the emf and ΔG for: Mg | Mg2+ (0.01 M) || Ag+ (0.001 M) | Ag at 298 K. E°(Mg2+/Mg) = -2.37 V, E°(Ag+/Ag) = +0.80 V, 1 F = 96500 C/mol.
**Ans:**
- E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode = 0.80 - (-2.37) = 3.17 V
- Reaction: Mg + 2Ag+ → Mg2+ + 2Ag, so n = 2
- E = E° - (0.059/n) log([Mg2+]/[Ag+]^2)
- = 3.17 - (0.059/2) log(0.01 / (0.001)^2) = 3.17 - 0.0295 × log(10^4) = 3.17 - 0.118 = **3.052 V**
- ΔG = -nFE = -2 × 96500 × 3.052 = -589036 J = **-589 kJ/mol**
- 2025 version ([Mg2+] = 0.100 M): log(0.1/10^-6) = 5, so E = 3.17 - 0.1475 = **3.02 V**

**Q18 ★ (2 marks, 2024)** Find the electrode potential of Fe2+ (0.01 M) / Fe at 298 K. E° = -0.45 V.
**Ans:** Fe2+ + 2e- → Fe, n = 2. E = E° - (0.059/2) log(1/[Fe2+]) = -0.45 - 0.0295 × log 100 = -0.45 - 0.059 = **-0.509 V**.

**Q19 ★ (3 marks, 2016)** Calculate the emf at 298 K for 2Cr + 3Fe2+ (0.1 M) → 2Cr3+ (0.01 M) + 3Fe. E°(Cr3+/Cr) = -0.74 V, E°(Fe2+/Fe) = -0.44 V.
**Ans:** E° = -0.44 - (-0.74) = 0.30 V; n = 6. E = 0.30 - (0.059/6) log([Cr3+]^2/[Fe2+]^3) = 0.30 - (0.059/6) log(10^-4 / 10^-3) = 0.30 - (0.059/6)(-1) = 0.30 + 0.0098 = **0.3098 V**.

**Q20 ★ (3 marks, 2020; 2023 similar)** Calculate ΔG° for Zn + Cu2+ → Zn2+ + Cu. E°(Zn2+/Zn) = -0.76 V, E°(Cu2+/Cu) = +0.34 V.
**Ans:** E° = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10 V. ΔG° = -nFE° = -2 × 96500 × 1.10 = **-212300 J = -212.3 kJ/mol**.
2023 version: Ni + 2Ag+ → Ni2+ + 2Ag, E° = 0.80 - (-0.25) = 1.05 V. ΔG° = -2 × 96500 × 1.05 = -202.65 kJ, so the maximum work is 202.65 kJ. log Kc = nE°/0.059 = 2 × 1.05/0.059 = **35.6**.

**Q21 ★ (3 marks, 2025; 2017 similar)** Λ°m of NH4Cl, NaOH and NaCl are 129.8, 217.4 and 108.9 S cm2/mol. Λm of 0.01 M NH4OH is 9.33 S cm2/mol. Find α.
**Ans:** By Kohlrausch's law, Λ°(NH4OH) = Λ°(NH4Cl) + Λ°(NaOH) - Λ°(NaCl) = 129.8 + 217.4 - 108.9 = 238.3 S cm2/mol. α = Λm/Λ°m = 9.33/238.3 = **0.039**.
(2017: acetic acid, Λ° = 349.6 + 40.9 = 390.5; α = 39.05/390.5 = 0.1)

**Q22 (2 marks, 2023, 2025)** State Kohlrausch's law and give two uses.
**Ans:** The limiting molar conductivity of an electrolyte is the sum of the limiting molar conductivities of its cation and anion: Λ°m = ν+ λ°+ + ν- λ°-. Uses: (1) to find Λ°m of a weak electrolyte; (2) to find the degree of dissociation α = Λm/Λ°m (and the dissociation constant).

**Q23 ★ (3 marks, 2024)** A cell with 0.05 M KCl (κ = 1.35 × 10^-2 S/cm) has R = 100 Ω. With 0.02 M AgNO3 it has R = 90 Ω. Find κ and Λm of AgNO3.
**Ans:** Cell constant G* = κ × R = 1.35 × 10^-2 × 100 = 1.35 cm^-1. κ(AgNO3) = G*/R = 1.35/90 = 0.015 S/cm. Λm = κ × 1000/C = 0.015 × 1000/0.02 = **750 S cm2/mol**.

**Q24 ★ (2 marks, 2015, 2025, SQP)** Why does conductivity decrease, but molar conductivity increase, on dilution?
**Ans:** Conductivity (κ) depends on the number of ions per unit volume. On dilution there are fewer ions per cm3, so κ **decreases**. Molar conductivity (Λm = κ × V) is for 1 mole of electrolyte. On dilution the total volume containing 1 mole increases (and a weak electrolyte ionises more), so Λm **increases**.

**Q25 (3 marks, 2017; 2019 similar)** A current of 2 A is passed through AgNO3 solution for 15 min. Find the mass of Ag deposited (Ag = 108).
**Ans:** Q = I × t = 2 × 900 = 1800 C. Ag+ + e- → Ag, so 96500 C deposits 108 g. Mass = 108 × 1800/96500 = **2.01 g**.

**Q26 (MCQ, SQP)** The charge needed to get 1 mol of metal M from M2O3 is: **Ans: 3F**, because M3+ + 3e- → M.

**Q27 (MCQ, 2026)** During electrolysis of aqueous CuCl2 with Pt electrodes: **Ans: Cl2 gas is released at the anode** (and Cu is deposited at the cathode).

**Q28 ★ (2-3 marks, 2016, 2020, 2024, 2025, 2026, SQP)** Batteries:
- (a) Primary vs secondary battery: In a primary battery the reaction occurs only once and it cannot be recharged (dry cell, mercury cell). A secondary battery can be recharged (lead storage battery).
- (b) Mercury cell: used in hearing aids and watches. It gives a constant voltage (1.35 V) because the overall reaction Zn(Hg) + HgO → ZnO + Hg has **no ion in solution** whose concentration changes.
- (c) Lead storage battery on **charging**: 2PbSO4 + 2H2O → Pb + PbO2 + 2H2SO4. On discharge H2SO4 is used up, so the specific gravity of the acid **falls**; on charging it **rises**.
- (d) H2-O2 fuel cell: anode 2H2 + 4OH- → 4H2O + 4e-; cathode O2 + 2H2O + 4e- → 4OH-; overall 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. Advantages: high efficiency (about 70%) and pollution-free (it only gives water). It was used in the Apollo space programme.
- (e) Cell uses (2025 MCQ): lead storage → inverter/automobile; mercury → wrist watch/hearing aid; dry cell → wall clock; fuel cell → Apollo.

**Q29 (1 mark, SQP)** Why are iron pipes connected to Mg? **Ans:** Mg is more reactive. It acts as a **sacrificial anode** and gets oxidised instead of iron, so the iron does not corrode.

### Chapter 3: Chemical Kinetics

**Q30 ★ (MCQ, SQP)** The half-life of a first-order reaction is 16 min. The time for 75% completion is: **Ans: 32 min** (75% = 2 half-lives).

**Q31 ★ (3 marks, 2015)** A first-order reaction takes 30 min for 50% completion. Find the time for 90% completion.
**Ans:** k = 0.693/30 = 0.0231 min^-1. t = (2.303/k) log([R]0/[R]) = (2.303/0.0231) log(100/10) = 99.7 × 1 ≈ **100 min**. (Shortcut: t90 = 3.32 × t1/2.)

**Q32 ★ (3 marks, 2026)** For the first-order reaction C2H5Cl(g) → C2H4(g) + HCl(g), the total pressure is 0.30 atm at t = 0 and 0.50 atm at t = 30 s. Find k (log 3 = 0.48).
**Ans:**
- Let the pressure of C2H5Cl fall by x. Total pressure = 0.30 - x + x + x = 0.30 + x = 0.50, so x = 0.20 atm.
- Pressure of C2H5Cl left = 0.30 - 0.20 = 0.10 atm
- k = (2.303/t) log(P0/P) = (2.303/30) log(0.30/0.10) = (2.303/30) × 0.48 = **3.68 × 10^-2 s^-1**

**Q33 ★ (3 marks, 2024; 2025 similar)** For 2NO + Br2 → 2NOBr:

| [NO] | [Br2] | Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.05 | 1.0 × 10^-3 |
| 0.05 | 0.15 | 3.0 × 10^-3 |
| 0.15 | 0.05 | 9.0 × 10^-3 |

Find the order, k, and the rate at [NO] = 0.4, [Br2] = 0.2.
**Ans:**
- [Br2] × 3 makes the rate × 3, so the order in Br2 = 1.
- [NO] × 3 makes the rate × 9, so the order in NO = 2.
- Rate = k[NO]^2[Br2] (overall order 3)
- k = 1.0 × 10^-3 / (0.05^2 × 0.05) = **8 L2 mol^-2 s^-1**
- Rate = 8 × 0.4^2 × 0.2 = **0.256 mol L^-1 s^-1**

**Q34 (2 marks, 2026)** 2A + B → 2C takes place in one step. How does the rate change if the volume is reduced to one-third? Does the order change?
**Ans:** Rate = k[A]^2[B]. Each concentration becomes 3 times, so the new rate = 3^2 × 3 = **27 times**. The order stays **3** (it does not change).

**Q35 (3 marks, 2019)** NH3 decomposes on Pt as a zero-order reaction with k = 4 × 10^-3 M/s. How long does it take for [NH3] to fall from 0.1 M to 0.064 M?
**Ans:** For zero order, t = ([R]0 - [R])/k = (0.1 - 0.064)/(4 × 10^-3) = 0.036/0.004 = **9 s**.

**Q36 ★ (3 marks, 2016; SQP similar)** For a first-order reaction, log k = 14.2 - (1.0 × 10^4 K)/T. Find Ea, and find k if t1/2 = 200 min.
**Ans:** Compare with log k = log A - Ea/(2.303 RT). So Ea/(2.303 R) = 1.0 × 10^4, giving Ea = 2.303 × 8.314 × 10^4 = 1.915 × 10^5 J = **191.5 kJ/mol**. k = 0.693/200 = **3.47 × 10^-3 min^-1**.

**Q37 ★ (2 marks, 2015, 2023, 2024, SQP)** What is a pseudo-first-order reaction? Give an example. (SQP: hydrolysis of acetyl chloride with (i) equimolar water, (ii) excess water.)
**Ans:** A reaction that has molecularity 2 but behaves as first order because one reactant is in large excess, so its concentration hardly changes. Example: hydrolysis of an ester, CH3COOC2H5 + H2O (excess) → CH3COOH + C2H5OH (H+ catalyst). Rate = k[ester]. SQP: (i) second order, (ii) first order.

**Q38 (2 marks, SQP, 2025)** "The rate of reaction stays constant during the reaction." Is this always true?
**Ans:** It is true only for a **zero-order** reaction (rate = k, independent of concentration). For first-, second- or other-order reactions, the rate decreases as the concentration of reactants decreases.

**Q39 (MCQ, 2024, 2026)** Graph and formula facts:

- The fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea is **e^(-Ea/RT)**.
- A catalyst lowers **Ea**. It does not change ΔG, ΔH or the equilibrium constant.

### Chapter 4: d- and f-Block Elements

**Q40 (MCQ, 2026)** Which of these is not a transition metal: Sc, Ag, Hg, Cu? **Ans: Hg.** Its d-orbitals are completely filled (5d10) in the atom and in its common ion Hg2+.

**Q41 ★ (5 × 1 marks, 2017, 2019, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). Give reasons:**
- (a) **Transition metals are good catalysts:** they show variable oxidation states, they form unstable intermediate compounds, and they provide a large surface area for adsorption (examples: Fe in Haber's process, V2O5 in the Contact process).
- (b) **Transition metals form complexes:** small size of the metal ions, high ionic charge, and vacant d-orbitals available for bonding.
- (c) **Transition metal ions are coloured:** they have unpaired d-electrons, which absorb visible light and undergo **d-d transitions**. Sc3+ (3d0) and Zn2+ (3d10) are colourless.
- (d) **Zn, Cd and Hg are not transition elements / have low melting points:** they have completely filled d10 orbitals in the ground state and in their common ions. With no unpaired d-electrons, metallic bonding is weak.
- (e) **Transition metals have high enthalpy of atomisation:** they have a large number of unpaired electrons, giving strong metallic bonding.

**Q49 ★ (2 marks, 2015, 2025, 2026, SQP). Crystal field theory:**

- In an octahedral field, the **eg orbitals (dx2-y2, dz2) are raised** because they point directly at the ligands and feel more repulsion (2026).
- d4 ion: if Δo < P (weak field), t2g3 eg1; if Δo > P (strong field), t2g4 eg0.
- d5 with Δo > P: t2g5 eg0 (SQP).
- [CoF6]3-: t2g4 eg2. [Co(NH3)6]3+: t2g6 eg0 (2026).
- Low-spin tetrahedral complexes are rare because Δt is small (about 4/9 Δo) and is less than the pairing energy (2023 A-R: answer A; 2025).

### Chapter 6: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

**Q52 ★ (3 marks, 2016, 2017, 2019, 2024, 2025, 2026, SQP). SN1 / SN2:**
- SN2 reactivity: 1° > 2° > 3° (steric hindrance). 1-Bromopentane reacts fastest by SN2. 2-Bromopentane is optically active. 2-Bromo-2-methylbutane (3°) is fastest in elimination and SN1 (2017).
- SN1 reactivity: 3° > 2° > 1°, depending on carbocation stability. Allylic/benzylic halides are very reactive (resonance-stabilised carbocation). (CH3)3C-I is more reactive than (CH3)3C-Br because C-I is weaker (2019).
- SN1 on an optically active halide gives **racemisation**: the planar carbocation can be attacked from both sides (2016, 2020, 2025).
- SN2 gives **inversion of configuration** (SQP MCQ: (-)-2-bromohexane → (+)-hexan-2-ol is SN2 with inversion).
- 2026 case:
  - (a) Rate = k[X], so X reacts by SN1 and is the 3° bromide, X = **(CH3)3C-Br** (2-bromo-2-methylpropane). Y is optically active and rate = k[Y][OH-], so Y reacts by SN2: Y = **CH3CH(Br)CH2CH3** (2-bromobutane, the only chiral C4H9Br).
  - (b) **Expected answer: X (SN1).** It goes through a flat (planar) carbocation, which the OH- can attack from either side. For a chiral substrate this gives a racemic mixture.
  - (c) **Y (SN2)** gives inversion of configuration, because OH- attacks from the back side, opposite the Br.
  - Teacher's note: (CH3)3CBr itself is not chiral, so the racemisation in (b) is the general SN1 rule. Write the reason (planar carbocation, attack from both sides) to get the mark.

**Q53 ★ (2-3 marks, 2016, 2024, 2025, 2026). Haloarenes are less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution.** Reasons:
- (i) **Resonance** gives the C-Cl bond partial double-bond character.
- (ii) The carbon is **sp2** (more s-character), so C-X is shorter and stronger.
- (iii) The phenyl cation is unstable.
- (iv) The electron-rich ring repels the nucleophile.
- Also: the C-Cl bond in chlorobenzene is **shorter** than in CH3Cl for reasons (i) and (ii).
- A -NO2 group at the ortho/para position **increases** reactivity: it withdraws electrons and stabilises the carbanion intermediate. So the nitro group activates; the methoxy group does not (SQP).

**Q60 ★ (2 marks, 2016, 2017, 2023, 2025, 2026, SQP). Distinguishing tests:**

- **Acid vs aldehyde** (propanoic acid vs propanal; benzoic acid vs ethyl benzoate): NaHCO3 gives brisk effervescence (CO2) with the acid only.

**Q61 ★ (3 marks, 2026, 2025, SQP). Give reasons:**

**Q64 ★ (1-2 marks, 2015, 2017, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026, SQP). Basicity:**

- **Benzene:** C6H5N2+Cl- + H3PO2 + H2O → C6H6 + N2 + H3PO3 + HCl
- **Coupling:** C6H5N2+Cl- + phenol (OH-) → p-hydroxyazobenzene (orange dye). This is the test that distinguishes aniline from methylamine/ethanamine: aliphatic amines give N2 gas, not a dye.
- **p-Bromoaniline:** aniline + (CH3CO)2O → acetanilide; + Br2/CH3COOH → p-bromoacetanilide; + H+/H2O → p-bromoaniline
- **Benzoic acid → benzylamine (SQP):** C6H5COOH + NH3 (heat) → C6H5CONH2; + LiAlH4 → C6H5CH2NH2

### Chapter 10: Biomolecules

**Q68 ★ (3 marks, 2019, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). Reactions of D-glucose:**
- **HI (long heating) → n-hexane.** Shows that the 6 C atoms are in a **straight chain**.
- **Br2 water → gluconic acid** (CHO → COOH). Shows the **aldehyde group**.
- **Conc HNO3 → saccharic (glucaric) acid**, HOOC-(CHOH)4-COOH. Shows a 1° alcohol group as well.
- **Acetic anhydride → glucose pentaacetate.** Shows **five -OH groups**.
- **HCN → cyanohydrin; NH2OH → oxime.** Show the C=O group.
- Glucose does not give the 2,4-DNP test or Schiff's test, and its pentaacetate does not react with NH2OH. This shows the free -CHO is absent: glucose exists in a **cyclic** form. (2026 A-R: A true, R false → C.)

**Q69 ★ (2 marks, 2019, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026). Linkages:**
- **Peptide linkage:** the amide link -CO-NH- formed between the -COOH of one amino acid and the -NH2 of another, with loss of water. It joins amino acids into proteins.
- **Glycosidic linkage:** an oxide link (C-O-C) between two monosaccharide units, with loss of water. It joins sugars (for example, C1-C4 α-glycosidic links in amylose, 2023).
- **Essential amino acids** cannot be made in the body and must come from food (valine, leucine, lysine). **Non-essential** ones are made in the body (glycine, alanine, glutamic acid). SQP: HOOC-CH2-CH2-CH(NH2)-COOH (glutamic acid) is **acidic and non-essential**.

**Q70 ★ (2 marks, 2016, 2020, 2025, SQP). Nucleic acids:**
- DNA vs RNA:
  - Sugar: DNA has 2-deoxyribose; RNA has ribose.
  - Bases: DNA has A, G, C, **T**; RNA has A, G, C, **U**.
  - Strands: DNA is double-stranded (double helix); RNA is single-stranded.
  - Role: DNA carries heredity (replication); RNA makes proteins.
- **Nucleoside** = base + sugar. **Nucleotide** = base + sugar + phosphate.
- The two DNA strands are **complementary** (not identical): A pairs with T (2 H-bonds) and G pairs with C (3 H-bonds), held by hydrogen bonds.
- The third component of DNA, besides the sugar and base, is **phosphoric acid** (2024).

**Q71 ★ (1 mark each, 2015, 2016, 2024, 2026). Vitamins:**
- Fat-soluble: **A, D, E, K**. They are stored in liver and adipose tissue.
- Water-soluble: **B group and C**. They must be taken daily because they are excreted in urine.
- Deficiencies:
  - A: night blindness, xerophthalmia
  - B1: beriberi
  - B12: pernicious anaemia
  - C: scurvy (bleeding gums)
  - D: rickets (bone deformities in children)
  - E: **increased fragility of RBCs and muscular weakness** (2026 MCQ)
  - K: increased blood clotting time

**Q72 ★ (2-3 marks, 2015, 2016, 2019, 2020, 2023, 2025, SQP). Carbohydrates and proteins, short facts:**
- Hydrolysis products:
  - **Sucrose** → glucose + fructose. It is **non-reducing** because the glycosidic bond joins both reducing groups (C1 of glucose and C2 of fructose). It is called **invert sugar** because sucrose is dextrorotatory, but the mixture formed on hydrolysis is laevorotatory: fructose (-92.4°) outweighs glucose (+52.5°).
  - **Maltose** → 2 glucose
  - **Lactose** → glucose + galactose (β-D-galactose + β-D-glucose)
- **Starch vs cellulose:** starch is made of α-D-glucose units (amylose: unbranched, C1-C4; amylopectin: branched, C1-C4 + C1-C6). Cellulose is a straight chain of β-D-glucose units (C1-C4).
- **Anomers:** cyclic forms of a sugar that differ only in the configuration at C1 (α and β glucose).
- **Fibrous vs globular proteins:** fibrous proteins are thread-like and water-insoluble (keratin, myosin). Globular proteins are spherical and water-soluble (insulin, albumin).
- Secondary structure: α-helix and β-pleated sheet. The α-helix is a feature of **polypeptides** (2023 MCQ).
- **Denaturation** (SQP: lemon juice in milk): a change in pH breaks the H-bonds, so the protein unfolds and loses its biological activity (the casein curdles). The primary structure stays the same.
- **SQP case:** a sugar that gives a positive Benedict's test (reducing) and gives only one monosaccharide on hydrolysis is **maltose**.

## 6. Quick revision sheet

### 6a. Formulas (learn these 20 and you can do every numerical)

**Solutions**
- Raoult's law: p = p° × x. Ideal mixture: P(total) = pA° xA + pB° xB
- Relative lowering of vapour pressure: (p° - p)/p° = x(solute) ≈ n2/n1
- ΔTb = i Kb m;  ΔTf = i Kf m;  π = i C R T
- Molar mass from ΔTf: M2 = (Kf × w2 × 1000) / (ΔTf × w1)
- Henry's law: p = KH × x. Higher KH means lower solubility.
- van't Hoff factor:
  - Dissociation: i = 1 + (n - 1)α
  - Association (dimer): i = 1 - α/2
  - Examples: NaCl, KCl → i = 2; CaCl2, MgCl2, Na2SO4 → i = 3; AlCl3, K3[Fe(CN)6] → i = 4

**Electrochemistry**
- E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode (both as reduction potentials)
- Nernst equation at 298 K: E = E° - (0.059/n) log Q, where Q = [products]/[reactants] (ignore solids)
- ΔG = -nFE;  ΔG° = -nFE° = -2.303 RT log K;  log Kc = nE°/0.059
- κ = G* / R (cell constant G* = l/A);  Λm = κ × 1000 / C (S cm2 mol^-1)
- Strong electrolyte: Λm = Λ°m - A√c
- Kohlrausch: Λ°m = ν+ λ°+ + ν- λ°-;  α = Λm / Λ°m
- Faraday: Q = I × t; mass = (M × I × t) / (n × 96500)

**Kinetics**
- Zero order: [R] = [R]0 - kt;  t1/2 = [R]0/2k;  unit of k = mol L^-1 s^-1
- First order: k = (2.303/t) log([R]0/[R]);  t1/2 = 0.693/k (independent of [R]0);  unit of k = s^-1
- Gas-phase first order: k = (2.303/t) log(P0 / (2P0 - Pt))
- Unit of k for order n: (mol L^-1)^(1-n) s^-1
- Arrhenius: k = A e^(-Ea/RT);  log(k2/k1) = (Ea / 2.303R)(1/T1 - 1/T2)

**Coordination**
- Spin-only magnetic moment μ = √(n(n+2)) BM. For n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: μ = 1.73, 2.83, 3.87, 4.90, 5.92.

**Must-know numbers:** log 2 = 0.3010, log 3 = 0.4771, log 4 = 0.6021, log 5 = 0.6990; 2.303RT/F = 0.059 V at 298 K; 1 F = 96500 C mol^-1; R = 8.314 J/K/mol = 0.0821 L atm/K/mol.

### 6b. Key definitions (1-mark favourites)
- **Molality (m):** moles of solute per kg of solvent. It does not change with temperature.
- **Molarity (M):** moles of solute per litre of solution.
- **Colligative property:** depends only on the number of solute particles, not on their nature.
- **Azeotrope:** a liquid mixture with the same composition in the liquid and vapour phase that boils at constant temperature.
  - Positive deviation gives a minimum-boiling azeotrope (95% ethanol + water).
  - Negative deviation gives a maximum-boiling azeotrope (68% HNO3 + 32% water, SQP).
- **Ideal solution:** obeys Raoult's law at all concentrations; ΔmixH = 0 and ΔmixV = 0.
- **Molecularity:** the number of species colliding in an elementary step. It is always a whole number and never zero.
- **Order:** the sum of the powers of concentration in the rate law. It can be 0 or a fraction.
- **Limiting molar conductivity (Λ°m):** the molar conductivity at infinite dilution (concentration → 0).
- **Fuel cell:** a cell that changes the energy of combustion of a fuel (H2, CH4) directly into electricity.
- **Corrosion:** an electrochemical process in which a metal is oxidised by air and moisture (rusting of iron).
- **Transition element:** an element with a partly filled d-subshell in the atom or in any common ion.
- **Ambidentate ligand:** a ligand that can bind through two different atoms (NO2-, SCN-, CN-).
- **Chelate:** a complex in which a polydentate ligand forms a ring with the metal (e.g. en, C2O4^2-, EDTA).
- **Racemic mixture:** an equal mixture of d and l forms. It is optically inactive because the two rotations cancel.
- **Enantiomers:** non-superimposable mirror images. They have the same mp, bp and reactivity, and **opposite** rotation of equal size.

### 6e. Diagrams to learn (describe and draw neatly)
1. **Vapour pressure-composition graphs:** a straight line for an ideal solution. With positive deviation the total-pressure curve lies **above** the line; with negative deviation it lies **below**.
2. **Λm vs √c:** for a strong electrolyte (KCl) it is a nearly straight line with a small downward slope; extrapolate it to c = 0 to get Λ°m. For a weak electrolyte (CH3COOH) it rises steeply at low concentration and cannot be extrapolated.
3. **Kinetics graphs:**
   - Zero order: [R] vs t is a straight line with slope -k.
   - First order: ln[R] vs t is a straight line with slope -k; log([R]0/[R]) vs t is a straight line with slope k/2.303.
   - Arrhenius: log k vs 1/T is a straight line with slope -Ea/(2.303R).
4. **Energy profile with catalyst:** two humps between reactants and products. The catalysed hump is lower (lower Ea); ΔH is the same.
5. **Crystal field splitting (octahedral):** five d-orbitals split into lower t2g (dxy, dyz, dxz), at -0.4Δo, and upper eg (dx2-y2, dz2), at +0.6Δo.
6. **Cis and trans [Pt(NH3)2Cl2]** (square planar): in cis the two Cl are next to each other; in trans they are opposite.
7. **Optical isomers of [Co(en)3]3+:** two mirror-image octahedra with three en "loops".
8. **Ring structure of α-D-glucopyranose:** a six-membered ring with O in the ring. The C1-OH points down (α).
9. **H2-O2 fuel cell:** porous carbon electrodes, concentrated aqueous NaOH, H2 in at the anode, O2 in at the cathode, water out.

## 7. Answer-writing tips (to collect step marks) and a 30-day timetable

### 7a. Tips
1. **Numericals:** write the **formula first** (½ mark), then substitute values with units (½ mark), then the answer **with units** (½ mark). Even a wrong final answer can still earn 1½-2 of the 3 marks.
2. **Nernst questions:** first write the cell reaction and **n**. Then calculate E°cell. Many marks come from these two lines alone.
3. **Organic "identify A, B, C":** write the structures **and** the reactions. Name each reaction ("Hofmann bromamide reaction") even if you are unsure of the product; a correct reagent or name can earn ½ mark.
4. **"Give reasons" (1 mark each):** use a keyword sentence. For example: "due to resonance", "due to the -I effect", "due to steric hindrance", "due to the stable half-filled d5 configuration", "due to H-bonding", "due to unpaired d-electrons".
5. **Coordination questions:** draw the orbital box diagram (3d, 4s, 4p). The examiner gives marks for the hybridisation and the number of unpaired electrons separately.
6. **MCQ and A-R:** never leave one blank. For A-R, first decide if A is true and if R is true, then ask "does R explain A?".
7. **Choose the easier OR option**, usually the theory one rather than the numerical, unless the numerical is a Nernst or ΔTf one you have practised.
8. **Start with Section A**, then go to the questions you know best. Write the question number clearly. Leave 10 minutes at the end to check units and signs.
9. Write equations with **arrows and conditions** (catalyst, temperature) above the arrow. Balanced equations score full marks.
