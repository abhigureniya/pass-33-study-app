3. The 33-mark plan (target about 40% = 32 marks in theory)

Main idea: You don't need every chapter. Lock in the 5 map marks, the short, repeated chapters in Political Science and Economics, and Nationalism in India. Attempt all 20 MCQs. A guess still has a 1-in-4 chance.

3a. Study order (easiest and highest return first)

  1. Maps (5 marks): a fixed list. Learn the History map (8 places) and about 15 Geography points that come again and again (dams, steel plants, ports, airports, nuclear plants). See Section 6f.
  2. Outcomes of Democracy + Power-sharing (Pol Sci): short chapters, and the same questions come almost every year.
  3. Political Parties + Federalism (1992 amendment): "functions of parties" and "1992 amendment" are asked nearly every year.
  4. Money and Credit + Development (Economics): formal vs informal credit, and sustainability of development, come back nearly every year.
  5. Sectors of the Indian Economy: public vs private, organised vs unorganised, tertiary sector.
  6. Nationalism in India (History): Non-Cooperation Movement, Civil Disobedience Movement, Dandi, Jallianwala Bagh. These also give you the 2 map marks.
  7. Agriculture + Water Resources (Geography): rabi/kharif, tea/coffee/rice/wheat conditions, dams, rainwater harvesting.
  8. Bonus: Print Culture (Gutenberg, Reformation) and Nationalism in Europe (Napoleonic Code, Marianne/Germania, Britain).

3c. How internal assessment helps you

  • IA is 20 marks, and most students get 16-20 if they submit everything on time. That is half the pass mark before the board exam even starts.
  • Submit the project (Consumer Rights is the easiest: COPRA 1986/2019, consumer rights, the ISI/Agmark/Hallmark logos, a real complaint case). Write it neatly with pictures, a bibliography and an acknowledgement.
  • Do the interdisciplinary project in class (silk routes / trade, transport lifelines, globalisation).
  • Keep your notebook complete for the portfolio. Attend the periodic tests, because even 3/5 helps.

5. Must-do question bank (72 questions)

★ = repeated most often, so learn these first. "Seen:" lists the years a question on this topic appeared in the papers read (the wording may be different). "SQP" = 2026-27 Sample Question Paper.

Part 1: Mixed MCQs (1 mark each, all four sections)

Q1. ★ (History) Which of these was a feature of the Napoleonic Code of 1804? Seen: 2019, 2025 A. It restored privileges based on birth B. It established equality before the law and secured the right to property C. It gave voting rights to all adults D. It made the Pope head of the state

Answer: B. The Code did away with privileges based on birth, gave equality before the law and secured the right to property.

Q2. (History) Who set up the Swaraj Party within the Congress? SQP A. Sardar Patel and Bipin Chandra Pal B. Tilak and Lala Lajpat Rai C. Nehru and Subhas Bose D. C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru

Answer: D. C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru wanted to return to council politics, so they formed the Swaraj Party.

Q3. ★ (History) Arrange these in order, from earliest to latest: SQP; print topic seen 2018, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2 I. Print culture created the conditions for the French Revolution. II. Martin Luther's writings began the Protestant Reformation. III. Menocchio reinterpreted the message of the Bible. IV. Gutenberg invented the printing press.

Answer: IV, II, III, I. Gutenberg's press came in 1448, Luther's 95 Theses in 1517, Menocchio in the 16th century, and the French Revolution in 1789.

Q4. ★ (History) Assertion (A): Gandhiji called off the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922. Reason (R): A peaceful demonstration turned violent at Chauri Chaura.

Answer: Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A. Gandhiji felt that satyagrahis needed more training before mass struggles.

Q5. (Geography) A steel company needs iron ore with the best physical properties for high-quality steel. Which belt is most suitable? SQP A. Maharashtra-Goa B. Odisha-Jharkhand C. Durg-Bastar-Chandrapur D. Ballari-Chitradurga

Answer: C. The Bailadila range in this belt has super-high-grade haematite with the best physical properties for steel-making.

Q6. (Geography) Which statements about placer deposits are correct? SQP (a) Found in valley floors and at the base of hills (b) Contain minerals that dissolve easily in water (c) Gold and platinum are found in them (d) Formed by deposition by running water

Answer: (a), (c) and (d). Placer minerals are those that are not corroded by water.

Q7. ★ (Geography) Which group has only rabi crops? Seen: 2018, 2019, 2024, 2026 A. Rice, maize, cotton B. Wheat, barley, peas, gram, mustard C. Watermelon, cucumber D. Jute, groundnut, soyabean

Answer: B. Rabi crops are sown in winter (October-December) and harvested in summer (April-June).

Q8. (Political Science) From a prudential viewpoint, which argument best supports power sharing? SQP A. It is morally correct B. It upholds democratic ideals C. It reduces the chances of social unrest D. It guarantees equal economic growth

Answer: C. Prudential reasons look at results: less conflict and more stability. Moral reasons look at the value of power sharing itself.

Q9. (Political Science) A party holds regular elections, but the same leaders hold power for decades and a small group takes all key decisions. This shows: SQP A. A federal party structure B. Lack of internal democracy C. Dynastic succession D. Strong ideological unity

Answer: B.

Q10. ★ (Economics) Assertion (A): Many poor households depend on informal sources of credit. Reason (R): Banks ask for collateral and documents, which poor people often do not have. Seen: 2018, 2019, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer: Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

Part 2: History (Section A)

Q11. ★ MAP (2 marks). Identify these places on the map of India: Seen: every paper read (INC session 2019, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2; Dandi 2018, 2019, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026E2; Jallianwala 2019, 2020, 2026) (a) The place where the INC session was held in September 1920. (b) The place where the Civil Disobedience Movement began. (c) The place where a massacre took place on 13 April 1919. (d) The place where Gandhiji led indigo peasants against the plantation system.

Answer: (a) Calcutta (Kolkata, West Bengal). (b) Dandi (Gujarat coast, south of Surat). (c) Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar (Punjab). (d) Champaran (north Bihar). Mark it with a dot and write the name on the line given.

Q12. ★ Explain any three changes Napoleon brought into the territories under his control. (3) Seen: 2019, 2025

Answer:

  1. The Civil Code of 1804 (Napoleonic Code) ended privileges based on birth, gave equality before the law and secured the right to property.
  2. He simplified administrative divisions and abolished the feudal system, so peasants were freed from serfdom and manorial dues.
  3. In towns, guild restrictions were removed. Transport and communication were improved, and he brought in uniform laws, standard weights and measures and a common currency, which helped businessmen.

Q13. ★ Explain the ideas of economic liberalism, or the steps taken towards economic unity, in 19th-century Germany. (3) Seen: 2020, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer:

  1. Economic liberalism meant freedom of markets and the end of state restrictions on the movement of goods and capital.
  2. Before unity, German areas had 39 states, each with its own currency, weights, measures and customs barriers. This made trade slow and costly.
  3. In 1834 the Zollverein, a customs union started by Prussia, removed tariff barriers and cut the number of currencies from over 30 to two. A network of railways also linked markets. Economic unity became a step towards national unity.

Q14. Explain what liberalism stood for politically in early 19th-century Europe. (3)

Answer: Liberalism comes from the Latin word liber, meaning free. For the middle classes it meant:

  1. Freedom for the individual and equality of all before the law.
  2. Government by consent, a constitution and a representative government elected by people.
  3. The end of autocracy and clerical privileges. Limit: voting rights were given only to property-owning men. Women and men without property had no vote.

Q15. ★ "The formation of the nation-state in Britain was not the result of a sudden upheaval." Explain. (5) Seen: 2024, 2026

Answer:

  1. Before the 18th century there was no British nation. People saw themselves as English, Welsh, Scot or Irish.
  2. The English nation grew in wealth and power and spread its influence over the other islands. In 1688 the English Parliament took power from the monarchy.
  3. The Act of Union (1707) between England and Scotland formed the "United Kingdom of Great Britain". In practice, England dominated Scotland.
  4. Scottish culture was suppressed: the Gaelic language was forbidden, Highlanders could not wear their national dress, and many were driven out of their land.
  5. Ireland, divided between Catholics and Protestants, was forcibly brought in in 1801 after a failed revolt led by Wolfe Tone (1798).
  6. A new "British nation" was spread through symbols: the Union Jack flag, the national anthem God Save Our Noble King, and the English language.

Q16. ★ Who were Marianne and Germania? What was the importance of the way they were shown? (2) Seen: 2023, 2026E2

Answer: They were female allegories (figures standing for an idea) of the nation: Marianne for France and Germania for Germany. Marianne wore a red cap and the tricolour, and her statues stood in public squares and appeared on coins and stamps. Germania wore a crown of oak leaves, because the German oak stands for heroism. They made the abstract idea of the nation easy for ordinary people to see and love.

Q17. ★ Why were the Balkans called "a big powder keg" after 1871? (3) Seen: 2018, 2024

Answer:

  1. The Balkans had many ethnic groups (Slavs, Greeks and others), and a large part was under the Ottoman Empire. As that empire broke up, romantic nationalism spread and each group wanted independence.
  2. The Balkan states were jealous of each other, and each wanted more territory at the cost of the others.
  3. Big powers (Russia, Germany, England and Austria-Hungary) competed for control of the region, which led to a series of wars and finally the First World War (1914).

Q18. Describe the Greek War of Independence. (3) Seen: 2020

Answer:

  1. Greece had been part of the Ottoman Empire since the 15th century. The struggle for independence began in 1821.
  2. Greek nationalists got support from Greeks living in exile and from many West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture. Poets and artists praised Greece as the cradle of European civilisation. The English poet Lord Byron raised funds and died in the war in 1824.
  3. The Treaty of Constantinople (1832) recognised Greece as an independent nation.

Q19. ★ Why did Gandhiji start the Non-Cooperation Movement? How was it to unfold? (5) Seen: 2018, 2020, 2024, 2025

Answer:

  1. In Hind Swaraj (1909) Gandhiji wrote that British rule survived only because Indians cooperated. If Indians refused to cooperate, British rule would collapse within a year.
  2. Causes: the Rowlatt Act (1919), the Jallianwala Bagh massacre (13 April 1919) and the Khilafat issue. Gandhiji wanted Hindus and Muslims to unite.
  3. Stages: first, the surrender of titles given by the government. Then a boycott of the civil services, army, police, courts, legislative councils and schools.
  4. Boycott of foreign goods, and a full civil disobedience campaign if the government used repression.
  5. The Congress accepted the programme at the Nagpur session in December 1920.

Q20. ★ Explain the economic effects of the Non-Cooperation Movement. (3) Seen: 2018, 2020, 2024, 2025

Answer:

  1. Foreign goods were boycotted, liquor shops were picketed and foreign cloth was burnt in huge bonfires.
  2. The import of foreign cloth halved between 1921 and 1922, and its value fell from Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore. Merchants refused to trade in foreign goods.
  3. People wore only Indian clothes, so the production of Indian textile mills and handlooms went up.

Q21. Why did the Non-Cooperation Movement slow down in the cities? (3)

Answer:

  1. Khadi cloth was more expensive than mill cloth, and poor people could not afford it.
  2. Boycotting British institutions needed Indian institutions to replace them, and these came up slowly.
  3. So students and teachers went back to government schools, and lawyers went back to government courts.

Q22. ★ How did different social groups take part in the Civil Disobedience Movement? (5) Seen: 2018, 2019, 2024, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. Rich peasants (Patidars of Gujarat, Jats of UP) were hit by the trade depression and falling prices, so they wanted lower revenue. They were disappointed when the movement was called off in 1931 without the revenue rates being revised.
  2. Poor peasants wanted unpaid rent to the landlord to be remitted. The Congress did not support "no rent" campaigns, so their relationship stayed uncertain.
  3. Business classes (FICCI, 1927; Purshottamdas Thakurdas, G.D. Birla) wanted protection against foreign imports and gave financial help. After the failure of the Round Table Conference they lost enthusiasm.
  4. Industrial workers took part in a small way, for example in Nagpur, by boycotting foreign goods.
  5. Women took part in large numbers: they joined the salt march, made salt and picketed liquor and foreign-cloth shops. The Congress was reluctant to give them positions of authority.

Q23. ★ Why did Gandhiji choose salt to start the Civil Disobedience Movement? (3) Seen (Dandi): 2018, 2019, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. Salt was eaten by both rich and poor, so it was one of the most essential items of food. It could unite everyone.
  2. The tax on salt and the government's monopoly over making it were very oppressive. Gandhiji's letter to Viceroy Irwin (31 January 1930) had 11 demands, and abolishing the salt tax was one of them.
  3. On 12 March 1930 he marched about 240 miles from Sabarmati to Dandi in 24 days, and broke the salt law on 6 April 1930. This started the movement.

Q24. ★ Explain the effects of the First World War on India. (3) Seen: 2023

Answer:

  1. Defence spending rose sharply. It was paid for by war loans and higher taxes: customs duties were raised and income tax was introduced.
  2. Prices doubled between 1913 and 1918, which caused hardship for common people.
  3. Forced recruitment of soldiers from villages caused anger. Crop failures in 1918-19 and 1920-21 caused food shortages, and an influenza epidemic killed 12 to 13 million people.

Q25. How did folklore, songs and symbols create a sense of collective belonging? (3) Seen: 2023

Answer:

  1. Image of Bharat Mata: Bankim Chandra wrote Vande Mataram, and Abanindranath Tagore painted Bharat Mata as a calm, divine figure.
  2. Folklore: Rabindranath Tagore collected ballads, nursery rhymes and myths. Natesa Sastri published The Folklore of Southern India. They saw these as the true picture of Indian culture.
  3. Flags: the Swadeshi flag (red, green, yellow) had 8 lotuses for 8 provinces. By 1921 Gandhiji's tricolour (red, green, white) had a spinning wheel in the centre. Carrying the flag became a symbol of defiance. (Also: reinterpreting history to show India's glorious past and to tell Indians to be proud.)

Q26. What was the Rowlatt Act? What happened at Jallianwala Bagh? (3) Seen (Jallianwala): 2019, 2020, 2026; SQP

Answer:

  1. The Rowlatt Act (1919) let the government detain political prisoners without trial for two years. Gandhiji started a nationwide satyagraha against it.
  2. On 13 April 1919, a crowd gathered in Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, some to protest and some for the Baisakhi fair. General Dyer blocked the exits and opened fire, killing hundreds.
  3. His aim was to create fear (a "moral effect"). Instead, it caused strikes, clashes and attacks on government buildings across north India.

Q27. ★ "Silk routes are a good example of pre-modern trade and cultural links." Explain. (2) Seen: 2024; SQP

Answer:

  1. The name shows the importance of Chinese silk cargoes that went west along these routes. Indian and South-East Asian textiles and spices also travelled, and gold and silver flowed from Europe to Asia.
  2. They also carried culture and religion: Christian missionaries, Muslim preachers and Buddhism spread along them. The routes existed before the Christian era and thrived until the 15th century.

Q28. ★ "The most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was not a conventional military weapon at all." Justify. (2) Seen: 2026; SQP

Answer:

  1. It was germs, such as smallpox, that the Spanish carried with them.
  2. America had long been cut off from the rest of the world, so its people had no immunity to these diseases. Smallpox spread deep into the continent and killed whole communities, even before the Europeans reached them. This made the conquest easy.

Q29. ★ How did Gutenberg develop the printing press? (3) Seen (print): 2018, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. Gutenberg, the son of a merchant, grew up on a large farm and saw wine and olive presses from childhood. He also learned to polish stones, became a master goldsmith, and learned to make lead moulds for trinkets.
  2. Using this knowledge, he adapted the olive press to make the printing press, and used moulds to cast metal type for the letters of the alphabet.
  3. By 1448 he had perfected the system. The first book he printed was the Bible: about 180 copies took 3 years, which was very fast for that time.

Q30. ★ How did print help the Protestant Reformation? (2) Seen (print): 2018, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. In 1517 Martin Luther wrote the Ninety-Five Theses, criticising many practices of the Roman Catholic Church, and posted them on a church door in Wittenberg.
  2. They were printed in large numbers and read widely, which led to the division of the Church and the Protestant Reformation. Luther said, "Printing is the ultimate gift of God and the greatest one."

Q31. ★ How did print culture create the conditions for the French Revolution? (3) Seen (print): 2018, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. Print spread the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers such as Voltaire and Rousseau, who attacked tradition, superstition and despotism and asked people to use reason.
  2. Print created a new culture of dialogue and debate. People questioned existing values and institutions.
  3. By the 1780s there was a lot of literature mocking the royalty (especially Marie Antoinette) and criticising its morals, which raised questions about the monarchy. (Note: print did not directly shape minds, but it opened up the possibility of thinking differently.)

Q32. ★ What was the Vernacular Press Act? Why was it passed? (3) Seen (censorship): 2018, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. After the revolt of 1857, the British grew afraid of Indian newspapers that criticised them and supported nationalism.
  2. In 1878 the Vernacular Press Act was passed, modelled on the Irish Press Laws. It gave the government wide powers to censor reports and editorials in the vernacular (Indian-language) press.
  3. If a report was judged seditious, the newspaper was warned. If the warning was ignored, the press could be seized and the printing machinery confiscated. Even so, nationalist newspapers grew in number.

Q34. ★ Explain the three main steps of resource planning in India. (3) Seen: 2020, 2025

Answer:

  1. Identification and inventory of resources across the regions of the country: surveying, mapping, and measuring their quality and quantity.
  2. Evolving a planning structure with the right technology, skills and institutions to put the plans into action.
  3. Matching resource development plans with overall national development plans.

Q35. ★ Write any three features of black soil. (3) Seen (soils): 2023, 2024, 2026

Answer:

  1. It is black in colour and is also called regur soil. It formed from lava rocks (Deccan trap) and covers Maharashtra, Saurashtra, Malwa, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh.
  2. It is ideal for cotton, so it is also called black cotton soil. It is made of very fine clay and holds moisture well.
  3. It is rich in calcium carbonate, magnesium, potash and lime, but poor in phosphoric content. It develops deep cracks in the hot season, which helps the soil get air.

Q36. ★ Write features of alluvial soil and laterite soil. (3) Seen (soils): 2023, 2024, 2026

Answer:

  • Alluvial soil: the most widespread soil, covering the northern plains (deposited by the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra) and the eastern coastal deltas. It is rich in potash, phosphoric acid and lime, and good for sugarcane, paddy and wheat. Old alluvium is bangar; new alluvium is khadar (more fertile).
  • Laterite soil: forms in areas of high temperature and heavy rainfall, through intense leaching. It is poor in humus, but with manures and fertilisers it is good for tea, coffee and cashew. It is found in Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Madhya Pradesh and the hills of Odisha and Assam.

Q37. What causes soil erosion? Suggest three ways to conserve soil. (3) Seen (soils): 2023, 2024, 2026

Answer:

  • Causes: deforestation, overgrazing, construction, mining, and wrong farming methods. Running water makes gullies (badlands); water flowing as a sheet causes sheet erosion; wind blows away the top soil.
  • Conservation: (1) Contour ploughing across hill slopes slows the flow of water. (2) Terrace farming on slopes (western and central Himalayas). (3) Strip cropping (grass strips between crops) and shelter belts (rows of trees) to break the force of the wind (western India).

Q38. ★ How do local communities help conserve forests? Give examples. (3) Seen in SQP case study

Answer:

  1. The Chipko Movement in the Himalayas: villagers hugged trees to stop them being cut.
  2. In Rajasthan, the Bishnois protect blackbuck, nilgai and peacocks. Villagers of Sariska fought mining and declared 1,200 hectares as the Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri.
  3. Joint Forest Management (Odisha, 1988) involves local communities in protecting and restoring degraded forests; in return they get forest produce. Sacred groves have also been protected by communities.

Q39. ★ Why is water scarcity growing in India even though water is renewable? (3) Seen: 2018, 2019, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. A large and growing population needs more water for drinking and for growing more food. Irrigated farming is the biggest user.
  2. Industrialisation and urbanisation use more water and more power. Cities have many housing societies with their own groundwater pumps.
  3. Over-exploitation of groundwater has lowered water tables. Water is also polluted by sewage, chemicals and fertilisers, so good-quality water is scarce even where there is plenty of water.

Q40. ★ Suggest two traditional rainwater harvesting methods for a drought-prone area. (2) SQP

Answer:

  1. Rooftop rainwater harvesting: rain from roofs is stored in underground tanks (tankas), as in Phalodi, Bikaner and Barmer in Rajasthan.
  2. Khadins and johads: rain-fed storage structures in Rajasthan where water stands and moistens the soil for growing crops. (Other examples: guls or kuls in the Western Himalayas, bamboo drip irrigation in Meghalaya, inundation channels in Bengal.)

Q41. What are multipurpose river projects? Why are they being opposed? (5)

Answer: These are dams that serve many purposes at once: irrigation, electricity, water supply, flood control, fishing and inland navigation. Nehru called them the "temples of modern India". They are opposed because:

  1. They stop the natural flow of rivers, so the riverbed gets more sediment and aquatic life suffers.
  2. They displace large numbers of people, who often get no proper compensation (Narmada Bachao Andolan, Tehri Dam Andolan).
  3. Big dams have sometimes caused floods instead of controlling them, because of excess sediment and heavy rain.
  4. Over-irrigation causes salinisation of soil, and benefits often go to rich landowners.
  5. They can cause earthquakes and water-borne diseases, and lead to pollution from excess use of water.

Q43. ★ Describe the conditions needed to grow tea, and name the tea and coffee areas. (5) Seen (agriculture): 2018, 2019, 2024, 2026; SQP

Answer:

  1. Tea is a plantation crop and a beverage crop, brought to India by the British.
  2. It needs a tropical or sub-tropical climate and deep, fertile, well-drained soil rich in humus and organic matter.
  3. It needs a warm, moist, frost-free climate all year, with frequent showers spread evenly over the year so that tender leaves keep growing.
  4. It is labour-intensive and needs plenty of cheap, skilled labour. It is processed in the tea garden itself.
  5. Tea areas: Assam, the Darjeeling and Jalpaiguri hills (West Bengal), Tamil Nadu and Kerala. Coffee: the Arabica variety from Yemen was first grown in the Baba Budan Hills. Today it is grown in the Nilgiris in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu.

Q44. ★ Explain any three types of farming in India. (3) Seen: 2018, 2019, 2024, 2026

Answer:

  1. Primitive subsistence farming: done on small patches with simple tools (hoe, dao, digging sticks) and family labour. It includes "slash and burn" (jhumming): land is cleared, crops are grown, and the farmer moves on when the soil loses fertility.
  2. Intensive subsistence farming: in densely populated areas. High doses of fertilisers and irrigation are used to get maximum output from a small plot.
  3. Commercial farming: high doses of modern inputs (HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides) are used to grow crops for sale. Plantation is one type, where a single crop such as tea, coffee, rubber or banana is grown on a large area.

Q45. ★ Why is it necessary to conserve energy resources? Suggest ways. (3) Seen: 2023, 2024, 2026

Answer: Energy is needed for every sector, but coal, oil and gas are limited and non-renewable, and burning them pollutes the air. Ways to conserve:

  1. Use public transport instead of private vehicles. Switch off lights and fans when they are not needed.
  2. Use power-saving devices such as LED bulbs and star-rated appliances.
  3. Use non-conventional sources such as solar, wind, biogas and tidal energy. Remember: "energy saved is energy produced".

Q46. ★ "Manufacturing industries are the backbone of economic development." Explain. (3) Seen: 2018, 2024, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. They help modernise agriculture and reduce people's heavy dependence on farming by creating jobs in the secondary and tertiary sectors.
  2. They help remove unemployment and poverty, and reduce regional disparities when industries are set up in tribal and backward areas.
  3. Export of manufactured goods expands trade and brings in foreign exchange. Countries that turn raw materials into a wide range of higher-value finished goods become prosperous.

Q47. Case-based (4), a practice passage modelled on the board flood-safety cases. Read: "During heavy rain, the water level in a dam rose quickly. The authorities opened the gates in advance and warned villages downstream to move to higher ground." Seen (flood-safety case): 2024, 2026 (a) Name one benefit of a dam, apart from flood control. (1) (b) Why must people downstream be warned before the gates are opened? (1) (c) Suggest two flood-safety steps for families living near rivers. (2)

Answer: (a) Irrigation, or hydroelectricity. (b) Released water suddenly raises river levels and can flood low-lying villages, so warnings save lives. (c) Move to higher ground with an emergency kit (water, food, torch, medicines, documents), and follow official alerts and avoid walking or driving through flood water.

Part 4: Political Science (Section C)

Q48. ★ Explain the steps Belgium took to accommodate its different communities. (5) Seen (power sharing): 2019, 2020, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2; SQP

Answer: (In Belgium, Dutch speakers are 59%, French speakers 40% and German speakers 1%. In Brussels, 80% speak French and 20% speak Dutch.)

  1. The Constitution says the number of Dutch- and French-speaking ministers in the central government must be equal. Some special laws need the support of a majority of each language group.
  2. Many powers of the central government were given to the state governments of the two regions. State governments are not under the central government.
  3. Brussels has a separate government in which both communities have equal representation.
  4. A "community government" is elected by people of one language community. It has power over cultural, educational and language matters.
  5. Result: Belgium avoided civil war and a split of the country.

Q49. ★ What majoritarian measures did Sri Lanka adopt? What was the result? (3) Seen (power sharing): 2019, 2020, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2

Answer: (Sinhalas are about 74% of the population; Tamils about 18%.)

  1. In 1956 an Act made Sinhala the only official language, ignoring Tamil.
  2. The government gave preference to Sinhala applicants for university places and government jobs. The Constitution said the state would protect and foster Buddhism.
  3. Result: Sri Lankan Tamils felt alienated. They demanded autonomy and an independent Tamil Eelam, and the conflict turned into a civil war.

Q50. ★ Describe the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies. (5) Seen: 2019, 2020, 2024, 2025, 2026, 2026E2

Answer:

  1. Horizontal sharing among organs of government (legislature, executive and judiciary). Each checks the others: a system of checks and balances.
  2. Vertical sharing among levels of government: central (union), state and local governments. This is federal government.
  3. Sharing among social groups, such as religious and linguistic groups: for example, the community government in Belgium and reserved seats for weaker sections in India.
  4. Sharing among political parties, pressure groups and movements: for example, coalition governments and the influence of pressure groups.

Q51. Give one prudential and one moral reason for power sharing. (2)

Answer: Prudential: it reduces conflict between social groups and ensures political stability. Moral: it is the very spirit of democracy: people affected by a policy have a right to be consulted.

Q52. ★ What makes India a federal country? (5) Seen (federalism): 2020, 2026; SQP

Answer:

  1. Two-tier government (Union and States), later a third tier of Panchayats and Municipalities.
  2. The Constitution divides powers into three lists. The Union List covers defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications and currency. The State List covers police, trade, commerce, agriculture and irrigation. The Concurrent List covers education, forests, trade unions, marriage, adoption and succession. Residuary subjects go to the Union.
  3. The basic division of power cannot be changed by one level alone. A change needs a two-thirds majority in both Houses of Parliament and the approval of at least half the state legislatures.
  4. Courts (the judiciary) settle disputes over the division of powers.
  5. Sources of revenue for each level are clearly specified. Linguistic states, the language policy (no single national language) and centre-state relations have also strengthened federalism.

Q53. ★ Explain the provisions of the 1992 constitutional amendment that strengthened local government. (5) Seen: 2018, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026; SQP

Answer:

  1. It is now constitutionally mandatory to hold regular elections to local government bodies.
  2. Seats are reserved in the elected bodies and in executive heads for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes.
  3. At least one-third of all positions are reserved for women.
  4. An independent State Election Commission in each state conducts panchayat and municipal elections.
  5. State governments must share some powers and revenue with local bodies. (Structure: Gram Panchayat (with the Gram Sabha), then Panchayat Samiti (block level), then Zilla Parishad (district). In towns: Municipalities and Municipal Corporations.)

Q54. ★ What is secularism? Explain the provisions that make India a secular state. (3) Seen: 2018, 2020, 2023, 2026

Answer:

  1. There is no official religion for the Indian state, unlike Buddhism in Sri Lanka or Islam in Pakistan.
  2. The Constitution gives all individuals and communities the freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion, or not to follow any.
  3. It prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion, and it allows the state to intervene in religious matters to ensure equality (for example, banning untouchability).

Q55. Describe the various forms communalism can take in politics. (3) Seen (secularism/communalism): 2018, 2020, 2023, 2026

Answer:

  1. Everyday beliefs: religious prejudice, stereotypes of other communities, and the belief that one's own religion is superior.
  2. Majoritarian dominance by the majority community, or the desire of a minority to form a separate political unit.
  3. Political mobilisation on religious lines, using sacred symbols and religious leaders to appeal to voters.
  4. Communal violence, riots and massacres (for example, at the time of Partition).

Q56. ★ "Elections are not all about caste." Give three arguments. (3) SQP

Answer:

  1. No parliamentary constituency has a clear majority of one single caste, so every candidate must win the trust of more than one caste.
  2. No party wins the votes of all the voters of one caste. People vote on many issues.
  3. Many constituencies have several candidates from the same caste, while many voters have no candidate from their caste. Also, ruling parties and sitting MPs often lose elections, which would not happen if caste decided everything.

Q57. ★ Describe the functions of political parties in a democracy. (5) Seen: 2018, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026; SQP

Answer: A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in the government.

  1. Contest elections: parties choose candidates.
  2. Put forward policies and programmes for voters to choose from.
  3. Play a decisive role in making laws.
  4. Form and run governments. Parties that lose play the role of the opposition, criticise the government and raise people's issues.
  5. Shape public opinion, and give people access to government machinery and welfare schemes.

Q58. ★ What are the challenges political parties face? (3) Seen: 2018, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer:

  1. Lack of internal democracy: power is concentrated in one or a few leaders, and there are no regular organisational elections.
  2. Dynastic succession: top positions go to members of one family.
  3. Growing role of money and muscle power, especially during elections.
  4. Lack of meaningful choice: the policies of major parties look very similar.

Q59. ★ How is democracy an accountable, responsive and legitimate government? (5) Seen (outcomes of democracy): 2018, 2019, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer:

  1. Accountable: citizens can choose their rulers and control them through free and fair elections held regularly.
  2. Decisions are taken through norms and procedures, after discussion. They may be slower, but they are more acceptable and effective.
  3. Transparency: a citizen has the right and the means to examine how decisions are made.
  4. Responsive: governments must attend to people's needs and demands, and there is open public debate on major policies.
  5. Legitimate: a democratic government is the people's own government. People everywhere want to be ruled by representatives they elect. Democracy may be slow and imperfect, but it is legitimate.

Q60. ★ "Democracy accommodates social diversity and promotes the dignity and freedom of citizens." Explain. (3) Seen (outcomes): 2018, 2019, 2020, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer:

  1. Democracies develop ways to handle differences and conflicts peacefully. The majority must work with the minority, and majority rule does not mean the rule of one religious or ethnic group.
  2. Democracy has strengthened the claims of women to equal treatment, and of the disadvantaged and discriminated castes to equal status and opportunity.
  3. It respects individual freedom and dignity. Even where discrimination remains, democracy gives people a legal and moral basis to fight it. (Limit: on economic growth and reducing inequality, democracies have not done as well as hoped. Dictatorships have had a slightly higher growth rate, but democracy is still preferred.)

Part 5: Economics (Section D)

Q61. ★ "Different people can have different, even conflicting, development goals." Explain with examples. (3) Seen: SQP

Answer:

  1. People have different life situations, so they want different things. A landless rural labourer wants more days of work and better wages; a rich farmer wants higher prices for crops.
  2. Goals can conflict: an industrialist wants more electricity from a big dam, but tribal people who would be displaced by it want to keep their land.
  3. Besides income, people also want equal treatment, freedom, security and respect. So what is development for one person may be destruction for another.

Q62. ★ What is per capita income? What are its limitations as an indicator of development? (3)

Answer:

  1. Per capita (average) income = Total income of the country / Total population. The World Bank uses it to compare countries.
  2. Limitation: an average hides inequality. Two countries with the same average income can have very different distributions.
  3. It does not show non-material things such as health, education, a clean environment or security. So the UNDP's Human Development Index (HDI) uses per capita income, education and health (life expectancy). Example: Kerala has a lower infant mortality rate than richer states because of better basic health and education facilities.

Q63. ★ Why is the issue of sustainability important for development? (3) Seen: 2018, 2023, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer:

  1. Development must meet the needs of the present without harming future generations.
  2. Resources are being overused: groundwater levels are falling in many parts of India, and non-renewable resources such as crude oil will run out.
  3. Environmental damage (pollution, soil erosion, global warming) does not respect state or national borders, so it affects everyone. Development that destroys the environment cannot last.

Q64. ★ Why is the tertiary sector becoming so important in India? (3) Seen: 2019, 2020, 2026

Answer:

  1. Basic services such as hospitals, schools, post and telegraph, police, courts, transport, banks and insurance are needed by everyone, and the government provides many of them.
  2. Growth of agriculture and industry increases the need for transport, trade and storage.
  3. Rising incomes create demand for services such as eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals and schools. New IT-based services have also grown fast. (Note: not all service jobs are good. Many people work in small shops and repair jobs with low pay and no job security.)

Q67. What is disguised unemployment? What is MGNREGA 2005? (3)

Answer:

  1. Disguised unemployment: more people are working than are needed, so if some are removed, output does not fall. Example: 5 family members working on a small farm that needs only 3.
  2. MGNREGA 2005: guarantees 100 days of employment in a year to every rural household that needs work. If work is not given, the government pays an unemployment allowance. One-third of the jobs are reserved for women.

Q68. ★ "Money is a medium of exchange." Explain. What are demand deposits? (3) Seen (demand deposit/cheque): 2026E2

Answer:

  1. In the barter system, both parties must want each other's goods: a double coincidence of wants. Money acts as an intermediate in exchange, so this is no longer needed.
  2. Demand deposits are deposits in bank accounts that can be withdrawn on demand. They share the features of money.
  3. A cheque is a paper instructing the bank to pay a certain amount from the account to the person named on it. So payments can be made without cash.

Q69. ★ Distinguish between formal and informal sources of credit. Why should formal credit expand? (5) Seen: 2018, 2019, 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer:

Q70. ★ What is the role of the RBI in supervising formal loans? (3) Seen (banks & RBI): 2020, 2023

Answer:

  1. The RBI issues currency on behalf of the government (only the RBI can issue currency notes).
  2. It makes sure banks keep a minimum cash balance out of their deposits.
  3. It checks that banks give loans to small farmers, small industries and small borrowers, not only to profit-making businesses. Banks must report how much they lend, to whom, and at what rate of interest.

Q71. ★ What are self-help groups (SHGs)? How do they help the rural poor? (3) Seen (cooperatives/SHGs): 2025

Answer:

  1. An SHG has 15-20 members, usually women from one area, who save regularly and pool their savings.
  2. Members get small loans from the group at reasonable interest, without collateral. After one or two years, the group can get bank loans.
  3. SHGs free the poor from moneylenders, help them start small businesses, and make women financially independent and self-confident. (Example: Grameen Bank of Bangladesh, founded by Muhammad Yunus.)

Q72. ★ What is globalisation? Explain the factors that enabled it. (5) Seen: 2024, 2025, 2026

Answer: Globalisation is the rapid integration or interconnection between countries through more foreign trade, more foreign investment by MNCs (companies that own or control production in more than one country), and the movement of technology and people.

  1. Technology (transport): faster and cheaper delivery of goods over long distances. Containers cut port handling costs, and air transport became cheaper.
  2. Information and communication technology: telecommunications, computers and the internet let a company in one country instantly share information and services with another (for example, a news magazine designed in Delhi and printed in Mumbai, or call centres serving clients abroad).
  3. Liberalisation of foreign trade and investment policy: removing trade barriers (such as taxes on imports) set by the government. Around 1991, India removed many barriers to foreign trade and investment.
  4. As a result, goods from around the world reached Indian markets, and Indian producers had to compete with world producers.

6b. History: key terms (1-mark favourites)

  • Nation-state: a state where most citizens develop a sense of common identity and shared history.
  • Absolutist: a monarchy with unlimited, unrestricted power.
  • Utopian: a vision of a society so ideal that it is unlikely ever to exist.
  • Plebiscite: a direct vote by which all the people of a region decide on a proposal.
  • Conservatism: a philosophy stressing the importance of tradition and established institutions.
  • Allegory: an abstract idea shown through a person or thing (Marianne, Germania).
  • Satyagraha: Gandhiji's method of truth and non-violence: an appeal to the conscience of the oppressor.
  • Begar: forced labour without payment.
  • Boycott: refusal to deal with people or to buy goods, as a form of protest.
  • Picket: a protest where people block the entrance to a shop, factory or office.

6c. Geography: key facts

  • Resources: potential (found but not yet used), developed (surveyed and used), stock (cannot be used because the technology is not available), reserves (part of stock usable with existing technology).
  • Agenda 21: signed at the Earth Summit, Rio de Janeiro, 1992, for sustainable development. Sustainable development: development without harming the environment or the needs of future generations.
  • Soils: alluvial (most widespread), black/regur (cotton), red and yellow (iron gives the red colour), laterite (leaching; tea, coffee, cashew), arid, forest.
  • Forests: reserved (more than half the forest land), protected (about one-third), unclassed. Project Tiger: 1973.
  • Dams on rivers: Salal on the Chenab, Bhakra Nangal on the Sutlej, Tehri on the Bhagirathi, Rana Pratap Sagar on the Chambal, Sardar Sarovar on the Narmada, Hirakud on the Mahanadi, Nagarjuna Sagar on the Krishna, Tungabhadra on the Tungabhadra.
  • Cropping seasons: Rabi (sown Oct-Dec, harvested Apr-Jun): wheat, barley, peas, gram, mustard. Kharif (sown with the monsoon, harvested Sep-Oct): rice, maize, jowar, bajra, tur, moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut, soyabean. Zaid (summer, between the two): watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables, fodder.
  • Minerals: iron ore types are haematite (most used industrially) and magnetite (finest, up to 70% iron). Coal types: peat, lignite (Neyveli), bituminous, anthracite (best quality). Mica comes mainly from Jharkhand (Koderma-Gaya-Hazaribagh belt). Bauxite is the ore of aluminium.
  • Industries: agro-based (cotton, jute, sugar), mineral-based (iron and steel, cement). Iron and steel is a basic industry. Jute industry is concentrated along the Hugli river.

6d. Political Science: key facts

  • Belgium: Dutch 59%, French 40%, German 1%; Brussels 80% French, 20% Dutch. Sri Lanka: Sinhala 74%, Tamil 18%; Sinhala-only Act 1956.
  • Federalism: "coming together" (USA, Switzerland, Australia) vs "holding together" (India, Spain, Belgium).
  • Three lists: Union, State, Concurrent; residuary powers go to the Union.
  • Local government: 1992 amendment; one-third of seats for women; State Election Commission; Gram Sabha = all voters of the village.
  • Feminist movement: movements demanding equality for women. Patriarchy: a system that values men more and gives them power over women.
  • Party systems: one-party (China), two-party (USA, UK), multi-party (India). National party: recognised by the Election Commission on its share of votes and seats in four or more states. Recognised parties get a unique symbol.
  • Outcomes of democracy: accountable, responsive, legitimate government; social diversity accommodated; dignity and freedom; weaker on economic growth and inequality.

6e. Economics: key terms and formulas

  • Per capita income = National income / Population. Used by the World Bank.
  • Infant Mortality Rate (IMR): children who die before the age of one, per 1,000 live births.
  • Literacy rate: the proportion of literate people in the 7-and-above age group.
  • Net Attendance Ratio: the share of children aged 14-15 attending school, out of all children in that age group.
  • HDI (UNDP): per capita income + education level + health (life expectancy).
  • GDP: the value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year (count only final goods, to avoid double counting).
  • Sectors: primary (agriculture, fishing, mining), secondary (manufacturing), tertiary (services).
  • Underemployment / disguised unemployment: more workers than needed. MGNREGA 2005: 100 days of guaranteed work.
  • Double coincidence of wants: both parties must want what the other has (the barter problem).
  • Collateral: an asset (land, building, vehicle, livestock, deposits) that the borrower pledges as a guarantee until the loan is repaid.
  • Terms of credit: interest rate, collateral, documentation and mode of repayment.
  • Debt trap: when a loan pushes the borrower into a situation from which recovery is very painful.
  • MNC: a company that owns or controls production in more than one country. Trade barrier: a restriction such as a tax on imports. Liberalisation: removing barriers set by the government.

7. Answer-writing tips (to collect step marks) and a 30-day timetable

7a. Tips

  1. Write each section in its own part of the answer book (A History, B Geography, C Pol Sci, D Economics), as the paper tells you.
  2. Never leave an MCQ blank. If you don't know, cut out the clearly wrong options and guess.
  3. Points, not paragraphs. One point = one mark. A 3-mark answer needs 3 clear points; a 5-mark answer needs 5. Underline the key word in each point (for example, Zollverein, collateral, one-third seats for women).
  4. Start with a one-line definition where you can (party, globalisation, collateral, sustainable development). It often earns the first mark.
  5. Use tables for "distinguish" questions (formal vs informal credit, public vs private, rice vs wheat). They are easy to mark and easy to write.
  6. Case-based questions: the answer to at least one sub-part is usually in the passage itself. Read the questions first, then the passage, and copy the key phrase.
  7. Map: attempt it first or last, but always attempt it. Write the name, not just a dot. Be neat; a messy map loses marks.
  8. Examples and dates earn marks: Belgium/Sri Lanka, Chipko, MGNREGA 2005, 1992 amendment, Dandi 1930.
  9. Stay within word limits (40/60/120/100) and save time for every question. A half-written answer gets more marks than a blank one.
  10. Time plan (3 hours): about 40 minutes per section, 15 minutes for maps, and 5 minutes to revise.
